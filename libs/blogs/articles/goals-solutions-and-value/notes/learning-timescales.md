# Human Learning Across Timescales

Human learning unfolds through overlapping processes ranging from seconds to
years. These ranges are approximate descriptions, not fixed biological
deadlines.

| Timescale | Predominant process |
| --- | --- |
| **Seconds to minutes** | The hippocampal system can rapidly bind the people, places, events, and relationships in a particular experience. |
| **Minutes to hours** | Synaptic consolidation begins stabilizing a newly encoded and initially fragile memory trace. |
| **Hours to days** | Replay during quiet rest and sleep reactivates recent experience; interactions among hippocampal and cortical systems can strengthen and reorganize the memory. |
| **Days to weeks** | Repetition, retrieval, and interleaving help integrate new information with existing knowledge and support generalization. |
| **Months to years** | Accumulated experiences contribute to durable semantic knowledge, schemas, skills, and reorganized long-term memories. |

## Complementary Learning Systems

The complementary learning systems account distinguishes between:

- a **fast hippocampal system** that can record the particulars of an
  experience after one or a few encounters; and
- a **slower neocortical system** that extracts regularities across experiences
  while reducing interference with established knowledge.

This is not a simple transfer from a temporary hippocampal store to a permanent
cortical store. The systems interact, cortical learning begins immediately,
and the hippocampus can continue contributing to older memories.

Prior knowledge also changes the timescale. Information that fits an existing
schema can be integrated much faster than information requiring a new
structure. In one set of animal experiments reviewed by McClelland and
colleagues, schema-consistent associations became supported by neocortical
networks within **48 hours**. This is an important exception to the simplified
claim that cortical learning is always slow.

## Sleep and Offline Consolidation

Recently encoded memories remain vulnerable to interference. Consolidation
continues after the learning event through multiple waves of cellular and
systems-level change. During non-REM sleep, coordinated slow oscillations,
sleep spindles, and hippocampal sharp-wave ripples are associated with the
reactivation of recent memories. This replay can help stabilize memories,
integrate them with existing knowledge, and sometimes transform what is
retained.

Sleep is therefore one recurring consolidation window, not the only time when
consolidation occurs. Quiet waking rest, later retrieval, additional
experience, and subsequent sleep periods can all continue changing a memory.

## Concise Formulation

> Human learning operates across timescales ranging from seconds to years: the
> hippocampus can encode a particular experience within moments, synaptic
> consolidation proceeds over hours, rest and sleep support replay over hours
> and days, and repeated experience gradually contributes to cortical
> knowledge, schemas, and skills over days, months, or years.
