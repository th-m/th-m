# Human Learning

## Research Synthesis

The scientific literature's strongest conclusion is that **human learning is
not one process**. It is a family of interacting systems that change
perception, knowledge, skills, expectations, values, and future behavior
through experience.

## What Counts as Learning?

A defensible scientific definition is:

> Human learning is a relatively durable, experience-dependent change in
> knowledge, skill, perception, expectation, or capacity for action.

Learning is distinct from memory: learning produces a change; memory preserves
or reconstructs some consequence of that change. It is also distinct from
immediate performance. Someone can temporarily perform well without retaining
anything, or learn something without displaying it immediately. Soderstrom and
Bjork emphasize that observable performance during training is often an
unreliable measure of long-term learning. See ["Learning versus performance: an
integrative review"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910388/).

## Multiple Learning Systems

| Form | What is learned | Important neural systems |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Episodic and declarative learning** | Events, facts, relationships, places, and contexts | Hippocampus and medial temporal lobe interacting with distributed cortex |
| **Semantic and statistical learning** | Concepts, categories, and regularities across experiences | Distributed cortical networks, with hippocampal and basal-ganglia involvement depending on the task |
| **Reinforcement learning** | Which actions or cues predict desirable and undesirable outcomes | Dopamine systems, striatum, orbitofrontal, and prefrontal networks |
| **Habit learning** | Automatic stimulus-response routines | Cortico-striatal systems, particularly sensorimotor portions of the striatum |
| **Perceptual learning** | More effective discrimination of sensory patterns | Sensory cortex together with attention and task-control networks |
| **Motor and skill learning** | Predictive control, coordinated actions, and practiced procedures | Cerebellum, basal ganglia, motor cortex, and sensory systems |
| **Emotional learning** | Salience, threat, safety, and affective associations | Amygdala interacting with hippocampal and cortical systems |
| **Social learning** | Actions, skills, conventions, and values learned from other people | Distributed systems for observation, action, reward, executive control, and social cognition |

These are regional emphases, not isolated learning centers. Human lesion
research demonstrates meaningful distinctions between declarative memory,
skills, habits, conditioning, and priming, but these systems normally interact.
See Squire and Dede, ["Conscious and unconscious memory
systems"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25731765/).

## What Physically Changes?

The dominant account holds that experience changes the effectiveness and
organization of neural circuits through several forms of plasticity:

- changes in synaptic strength, including long-term potentiation and
  depression;
- formation, elimination, and stabilization of synaptic connections;
- changes in neuronal excitability;
- altered coordination among distributed neural populations;
- neuromodulation by dopamine, noradrenaline, acetylcholine, and other systems;
  and
- slower structural changes, potentially including myelin remodeling.

Synaptic plasticity has strong experimental support, but the claim that
memories reside exclusively in altered synaptic weights is not established.
Learning probably depends on several interacting cellular and network
mechanisms. Relevant reviews include:

- Kandel, Dudai, and Mayford, ["The molecular and systems biology of
  memory"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24679534/).
- Magee and Grienberger, ["Synaptic Plasticity Forms and
  Functions"](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-neuro-090919-022842).
- Xin and Chan, ["Myelin plasticity: sculpting circuits in learning and
  memory"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41583-020-00379-8).

Much of the finest-grained causal evidence comes from nonhuman animals. Human
evidence relies more heavily on lesions, behavior, pharmacology,
electrophysiology, and neuroimaging, so mappings from cellular mechanisms to
complex human learning remain partly inferential.

## Fast Experience and Slow Generalization

An influential model proposes complementary learning systems:

- The **hippocampal system** can rapidly bind a particular event or
  relationship.
- **Neocortical systems** gradually integrate regularities across many
  experiences, supporting categories, schemas, and general knowledge.
- Reactivation and replay allow recent information to interact with established
  knowledge without simply overwriting it.

Prior knowledge can accelerate this process. Information that fits an existing
schema may be integrated quickly, while surprising information can receive
stronger episodic encoding. But schemas can also distort new evidence toward
what someone already expects. See McClelland, McNaughton, and Lampinen,
["Integration of new information in memory: new insights from a complementary
learning systems perspective"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7209926/).

The exact division of labor remains debated. The hippocampus itself can extract
some regularities, and researchers disagree about whether memories are
transferred, transformed, or continually reorganized.

## Learning Is Selective and Active

Humans do not indiscriminately absorb every available input. Learning is gated
by:

- attention;
- present goals;
- novelty and surprise;
- emotional and physiological significance;
- prior knowledge;
- expected reward;
- curiosity and uncertainty; and
- opportunities to act and observe consequences.

Humans can also influence their own training data: they look around, ask
questions, manipulate objects, test hypotheses, and seek information. Research
distinguishes task-directed information sampling from more open-ended,
curiosity-driven exploration. See Gottlieb and Oudeyer, ["Towards a
neuroscience of active sampling and
curiosity"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30397322/).

This means the draft's statement that "humans cannot turn off their inputs" is
too broad. A better claim is:

> Humans remain embedded in a continuing stream of bodily, environmental, and
> social experience, but attention, motivation, and prior knowledge strongly
> determine what produces lasting learning.

## Feedback, Prediction, and Consequences

Learning is often strongest when expectations encounter informative outcomes.

Dopaminergic activity can carry signals resembling reward-prediction errors—the
difference between expected and received outcomes—which help update values and
action policies. But dopamine also contributes to motivation, movement,
salience, and other functions; it should not be described as a single-purpose
learning signal. See Schultz, ["Dopamine reward prediction-error signalling: a
two-component response"](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn.2015.26).

Motor learning combines several kinds of signal:

- **Sensory error:** what happened versus what was intended.
- **Reinforcement:** whether the outcome was successful.
- **Observation:** what happened when someone else acted.
- **Use-dependent learning:** frequently repeated actions becoming easier or
  more likely.

See Wolpert, Diedrichsen, and Flanagan, ["Principles of sensorimotor
learning"](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn3112).

Feedback generally improves learning, but its content and timing matter. A
meta-analysis of 435 studies found a medium average benefit alongside
substantial variation between kinds of feedback. See Wisniewski, Zierer, and
Hattie, ["The Power of Feedback Revisited: A Meta-Analysis of Educational
Feedback Research"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32038429/).

## Learning Can Be Conscious or Unconscious

People can deliberately memorize a rule, practice a skill, or revise a belief.
They also learn without being able to articulate what changed.

Implicit and statistical learning allow people—including infants—to detect
regularities in sounds, visual sequences, actions, and language. This is
important for the draft because it means **human learning does not always
involve consciously experienced meaning**. See Batterink, Paller, and Reber,
["Understanding the Neural Bases of Implicit and Statistical
Learning"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7723461/).

A defensible contrast is therefore not:

> Humans learn meaning; machines learn patterns.

Humans also learn patterns, associations, and reward statistics. The stronger
claim is:

> Human learning integrates statistical regularities with embodiment,
> episodic experience, action, affect, goals, social relationships, and
> cultural interpretation.

## Humans Learn From Other Humans

Observation, imitation, testimony, teaching, and language let people learn
without personally rediscovering everything.

Social learning uses ordinary learning mechanisms—attention, action mapping,
reward, and memory—alongside systems for interpreting other people's intentions
and reliability. It enables cumulative culture: improvements can be preserved
and extended across generations. See Olsson, Knapska, and Lindstrom, ["The
neural and computational systems of social
learning"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32221497/).

This is a major feature of human learning, but it is not automatically
beneficial. Social learning also transmits errors, prejudice, superstition, and
maladaptive norms.

## Practice and Consolidation

Several findings are particularly well supported:

- **Retrieval practice:** Attempting to recall something strengthens later
  access more effectively than passive restudy in many conditions. See
  McDermott, ["Practicing Retrieval Facilitates
  Learning"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33006925/).
- **Spacing:** Distributing encounters over time generally produces better
  long-term retention than massing them together. A major meta-analysis covered
  317 experiments. See Cepeda et al., ["Distributed practice in verbal recall
  tasks: A review and quantitative
  synthesis"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/).
- **Interleaving and varied practice:** Comparing different problem types can
  improve discrimination and transfer, although benefits depend on the material
  and learner.
- **Corrective feedback:** Mistakes become useful when the learner receives
  information capable of changing the next attempt.
- **Sleep and offline rest:** Reactivation during sleep contributes to
  stabilization and reorganization of some memories. See Rasch and Born,
  ["About sleep's role in
  memory"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23589831/).

Conditions that make practice feel easy can improve immediate performance
without producing durable retention. Conversely, appropriately difficult
retrieval or varied practice can impair fluency during training while improving
later access. Difficulty is beneficial only when it remains manageable;
overload is not a desirable difficulty.

## Important Limitations

Human learning is powerful but not automatically rational:

- People preferentially encode salient information, not necessarily truthful
  information.
- Reward can reinforce maladaptive behavior.
- Stress can strengthen emotional or habitual learning while impairing flexible
  retrieval.
- Existing schemas can obstruct contradictory evidence.
- Skills often remain specific to the practiced context.
- Far transfer from cognitive training is usually much weaker than improvement
  on the trained task.
- People frequently misjudge how well they have learned something.
- Repetition can increase familiarity and confidence without establishing
  truth.

Stress can even shift behavior from flexible hippocampal strategies toward more
rigid striatal habits. See Vogel and Schwabe, ["Learning and memory under
stress: implications for the
classroom"](https://www.nature.com/articles/npjscilearn201611).

## Major Scientific Gaps

The literature does not yet fully explain:

1. How a particular concept, skill, or episode is physically represented across
   cells and circuits.
2. How synaptic, cellular, structural, and systems-level changes combine into
   one learned capacity.
3. How the brain assigns credit when an outcome follows a long sequence of
   thoughts and actions.
4. How rapid episodic learning becomes abstract, compositional knowledge.
5. How competing learning systems decide which one controls behavior.
6. How sleep selects which experiences to preserve, transform, or forget.
7. Why some training generalizes while other training remains narrowly
   task-specific.
8. How conscious instruction and implicit learning interact.
9. How bodily state, emotion, and social context alter what is learned.
10. How human cultural learning changes the organization of individual
    cognition.

## Defensible Formulation for the Article

> Human learning is an ongoing but selective process through which experience
> produces durable changes in perception, knowledge, skill, expectation, and
> action. It is implemented by multiple interacting systems: rapid episodic
> learning, gradual extraction of regularities, reinforcement and habit
> formation, perceptual and motor adaptation, and observation and instruction
> from other people. Attention, curiosity, emotion, bodily needs, and present
> goals influence what is learned, while practice, retrieval, feedback, sleep,
> and repeated use determine what persists. This flexibility allows people to
> learn throughout life, but it also makes learning fallible: humans acquire
> biases, false beliefs, and maladaptive habits through many of the same
> mechanisms that produce expertise.
