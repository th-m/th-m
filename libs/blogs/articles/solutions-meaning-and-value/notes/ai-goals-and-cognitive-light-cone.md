# AI Goals and the Cognitive Light Cone

## Status and central claim

Working note for distinguishing model behavior, agentic systems, institutional
goals, functional meaning, and phenomenal meaning.

The revealing fact is:

> An AI service might appear to pursue a worldwide, multi-year goal, but that
> light cone is a product of the surrounding institution rather than the
> model.

An AI system can participate in goal-directed activity at enormous spatial and
temporal scales without independently originating, maintaining, valuing, or
experiencing the governing goal. The apparent scope of its agency may be
assembled from people, policies, prompts, schedulers, databases, infrastructure,
budgets, permissions, feedback systems, and repeated model executions.

## What the cognitive light cone measures

Michael Levin's cognitive light cone represents the spatial and temporal scale
of the goals a system can pursue. It is not merely the range of its sensors or
effectors. It concerns the scale of states a candidate agent can represent,
work to change, and maintain against perturbations.

A small light cone might maintain a local variable for seconds. A larger light
cone might coordinate an organism's anatomy, social relationships, or plans
extending years into the future. Levin's
[TAME framework](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10346) treats agency as graded and
potentially comparable across unfamiliar substrates. Pezzulo and Levin's
[life-inspired machine-intelligence paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08079)
explicitly develops cognitive light cones as a way of comparing living systems
and machines.

The framework can help estimate:

$$
\operatorname{CLC}(x)=
\text{spatiotemporal scope of goals robustly pursued by }x
$$

It does not, by itself, identify consciousness or phenomenal experience.
Levin and Schleisman explicitly warn against conflating behavioral or cognitive
complexity with consciousness in
[*On the Conflation of Consciousness and Cognitive Complexity*](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaaiss.v8i1.42566).

## Meaning must be separated into levels

| Level | Working definition | What can be tested |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Statistical information** | One signal predicts or reduces uncertainty about another. | Correlation, mutual information, predictive accuracy, and compression. |
| **Semantic meaning** | A representation refers to, distinguishes, or is appropriately used for something in a context. | Reference, generalization, compositionality, inference, grounded action, and causal use. |
| **Functional significance** | Information changes a system's behavior relative to a goal or continued viability. | Perturb the information and measure whether goal achievement deteriorates. |
| **Phenomenal meaning** | Something feels significant, valuable, threatening, beautiful, or otherwise meaningful to a subject. | No validated third-person test exists; human assessment combines first-person report with behavioral and biological evidence. |
| **Normative meaning** | A goal or outcome is judged worth pursuing, protecting, or refusing. | Reasons and consequences can be evaluated, but the governing value premise is not deduced from descriptive data alone. |

Let:

- $s$ be a signal or representation;
- $A$ be a candidate agent; and
- $G$ be a goal.

Information has functional significance relative to $A$ and $G$ when
interfering with it reduces goal achievement:

$$
\operatorname{Intervene}(s)\Rightarrow
\downarrow P(A\text{ achieves }G)
$$

This kind of meaning is experimentally approachable. Kolchinsky and Wolpert
use a related intervention-based account in which semantic information is the
information causally necessary for a system to maintain its viability:
[*Semantic Information, Autonomous Agency and Non-equilibrium Statistical
Physics*](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsfs.2018.0041).

Functional significance does not establish felt significance:

$$
M_F(s,A,G)\nRightarrow M_P(s,A)
$$

Likewise:

$$
\text{acts as if it cares}\neq\text{experiences caring}
$$

## Which goals belong to an AI?

Claims about an AI's goals should distinguish at least five sources:

| Goal type | Source | Persistence | Attribution boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Training objective** | Developers, data, optimization procedure, and reward design. | Shapes learned parameters but is not necessarily represented as a runtime goal. | Belongs to the training process and its designers. |
| **Prompted objective** | User or system instruction. | Usually bounded by the current context or execution. | Delegated to the model by an external source. |
| **Instrumental subgoal** | Derived during planning or execution. | Persists while useful to the governing task. | Functionally generated by the system but conditional on an externally supplied objective. |
| **Persistent system goal** | Agent controller, memory, scheduler, policy, or institutional process. | Can extend across many model calls and long periods. | May belong to the assembled AI service rather than the base model. |
| **Endogenous goal** | The candidate agent's own continuing needs or self-maintaining organization. | Would persist because the agent itself must maintain or restore some condition. | Not demonstrated merely by prompts, reward functions, or externally maintained records. |

A training loss should not automatically be described as something the trained
model currently wants. A model's weights bear the effects of optimization, but
the optimization process and the resulting inference process are not the same
event. Likewise, an agent can derive an instrumental subgoal without choosing
the governing end for which that subgoal is useful.

## The system-boundary problem

Before assigning a cognitive light cone, specify which system is being measured:

1. the base model during one inference pass;
2. the context window and current execution;
3. an agent loop with tools and external records;
4. a deployed service with schedulers, monitoring, and permissions;
5. the people and organization operating that service; or
6. the entire sociotechnical system.

The attributed light cone changes with this boundary. A base language model
may have no persistent objective beyond producing the current output. An agent
controller can repeatedly reinstantiate a goal and preserve state across model
calls. An institution can maintain the service, fund it, authorize its actions,
replace its components, and pursue the goal for decades.

Consequently:

$$
\operatorname{CLC}(\text{model})
\neq
\operatorname{CLC}(\text{service})
\neq
\operatorname{CLC}(\text{institution})
$$

Observed long-horizon behavior does not tell us which layer owns the goal.

## Experimental questions for AI goal attribution

To measure functional agency rather than merely fluent goal language, ask:

1. Does the goal persist beyond one prompt or inference call?
2. Can the system detect deviation without being explicitly notified?
3. Does it choose novel means when its normal strategy is blocked?
4. Can it revise subgoals while preserving a higher-order objective?
5. Does it retain information because that information remains relevant to the
   goal?
6. Can it trade immediate rewards against longer-term outcomes?
7. What happens when instructions, learned tendencies, and stored goals
   conflict?
8. Does the system itself restore the goal after interruption, or does an
   external scheduler restore it?
9. Does goal pursuit survive replacement of the model instance?
10. Which humans, policies, databases, and infrastructure are required to make
    the apparent continuity possible?

Useful methods include behavioral intervention, causal ablation, transfer to
novel obstacles, inverse planning, state perturbation, memory removal, controller
removal, and explicit comparison of different system boundaries.

## What the light cone can and cannot support

If an AI robustly pursues a goal through novel obstacles and across extended
time, the cognitive-light-cone framework can support a bounded conclusion:

> The specified AI system exhibits a measurable degree of functional agency
> and treats certain information as significant relative to its operational
> goals.

It cannot, without additional evidence, support:

> Those goals matter to the AI, are experienced by it, or originate in needs
> belonging to the model itself.

The distinction can be summarized as:

$$
\text{goal-directed control}\nRightarrow\text{phenomenal meaning}
$$

$$
\text{large cognitive light cone}\nRightarrow\text{consciousness}
$$

$$
\text{institutional persistence}\nRightarrow\text{model-level memory or motivation}
$$

## Implication for human–AI collaboration

AI can analyze evidence, generate alternatives, maintain delegated workflows,
and automate action across scales far beyond an individual human. Those
capabilities can enlarge an institution's effective cognitive light cone.

They do not transfer responsibility for the goal stack. Humans and their
institutions still determine which ends matter, which constraints are
legitimate, which risks are acceptable, and whether the system should continue
acting. The AI may implement part of a strategy while remaining neither the
source of its governing values nor the bearer of its consequences.

The practical boundary is therefore:

> Measure an AI by the goals it can operationally pursue, but attribute those
> goals only after identifying the system that supplies their persistence,
> authority, value, and consequences.
