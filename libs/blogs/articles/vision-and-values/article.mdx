<Section index="01" title="AI has Hidden Priorities">

<Quote plain>

**“Hey AI, Optimize this plan, find all the gaps and ensure validation checks are in place.”**

</Quote>

Nine hours later it returned a huge mess, hundreds of validation checks and hallucinated product details.  Ultimately, the prompt failed and the output plan was completely unusable.

Why doesn't the AI know what `optimize` means? What fool tool interprets `find all the gaps` and treats every possible omission as equally important?

What I actually wanted was  a bit of common sense. Find the *gaps that affect outcomes*. Ensure *validation checks between boundaries*. Even with those clarifications in the prompt, research suggests it would only effect output two percent of the time.

<Callout label="Core thesis" emphasis>

**Strategy negotiates trade-offs between competing values, risks, resources, and time horizons. It decides what to pursue, what to protect, what to sacrifice, and when to change course. AI cannot meaningfully author the values that govern the strategy, yet it will implicitly apply it's own values**

</Callout>

<aside className="goals-article__research" aria-label="External research">

 In one [study of seven strategic tradeoffs](https://hbr.org/2026/03/researchers-asked-llms-for-strategic-advice-they-got-trendslop-in-return) researchers identified different levels of influence on AI's strategic recommendations:

* Changing the wording or asking the model to reason harder changed its answer in **fewer than 2% of cases**.
* Adding relevant company information changed its answer in **11% of cases**.
* Merely reversing the order of the choices changed its answer in **19% of cases**.


</aside>

The article shows that AI favored the same strategic positions despite adding relevant information that would encourage alternatives. Worse than that, the biggest factor affecting it's response was the way the choice was presented.

AI is biased. It comes baked with it's own values and strategic preferences. This bias is baked into the AI model from the beginning , starting with the training data,  pre-training, fine-tuning, system prompts, and the harnesses.  Furthermore, AI is a mechanical and mathmatical process order of input.

</Section>

<Section index="02" title="What a Language Model Carries">

<LlmCompressionDetail>An LLM is a compressed statistical model</LlmCompressionDetail>{" "}patterned from human language. Its value comes from the relationships it has learned among words, concepts, and contexts. Those relationships can support impressive inference, planning, and tool use. They do not, by themselves, establish that the model has lived through what its language describes.

I do not want to undersell this capability. It is astonishing that learned relationships among symbols can support so much useful work. But the available language remains incomplete and historically situated, and the model's behavior is further shaped by training objectives, post-training, system instructions, tools, and the surrounding harness.

<Section index="03" title="What Language Leaves Out">

Where is the limit of this language-compression and prediction process? A sufficiently capable system could produce an extraordinary answer to “What is my purpose?” It could describe your history, compare philosophies, identify tensions, and predict which answer you might accept. Even a perfect description, however, would not be the same as occupying your point of view or intending a purposeful life for you.

I will call the distance between a complete description and a lived point of view the **qualitative gap**. Philosophers use <Term definition="The felt, first-person qualities of experience—what pain, relief, color, trust, embarrassment, or delight are like for the subject.">qualia</Term> for the qualities on the experiential side of that gap. Current AI can describe qualia and predict reports about them; we do not have validated evidence that ordinary language models experience them. [AI Consciousness Is Incoherent](/writing/ai-consciousness-is-incoherent) develops that evidentiary boundary in detail.

<Callout label="The qualitative gap">

**AI can know everything recorded about your customer and still never have met your customer.**

**It can know everything documented about your team and still not know whether Alicia is pronounced _ah-LEE-sha_ or _ah-lee-SEE-ah_.**

</Callout>

These examples expose related but different gaps. Alicia's pronunciation is not qualia; it is tacit, relational context that nobody made available to the system. The irritation of being repeatedly misnamed, the trust created when someone remembers, or the relief a customer feels when a painful workflow disappears are qualitative. The common lesson is that complete coverage of recorded facts is not complete acquaintance with a person.

I like to imagine this situated knowing as the edges between objective facts: relationships, history, stakes, and felt consequences that determine what the facts mean to someone.

<Asset id="relational-knowing-figure" />

For this article, let’s define a value statement as an expression of how something matters to someone. These statements are shaped by subjective experience and individual preferences, reflecting personal desires, fears, and sources of meaning. “Jon earns $72,000” describes his salary. “Jon feels underpaid” expresses how he evaluates that salary. The second statement tells us something about what matters to Jon, but not yet what change would help him or what he would sacrifice to get it.

In linguistics, a <Asset id="morpheme-glossary" /> is a minimal unit of meaning. The word `pain` contains one morpheme.

<Asset id="morpheme-to-token-motif" />

Language is itself a compression tool. We encapsulate meaningful experience in shared terms and morphemes, but the compression is not lossless.

When someone says “that is painful,” the listener can only partially reconstruct the experience through context, empathy, and shared history. The same term carries different reference points. My wife calls an ice bath painful; I call it exhilarating. She calls gardening relaxing; I call it painful. Each person's network of associations around `pain` is different, and the felt quality is not exhausted by the word.

<Flow>experience → language → recording → training data</Flow>

Before going further into consciousness, two practical consequences matter here:

- Value statements get distorted in normalization.
- Value statements become meaningless without intent.

### The Distorted Average.
<Asset id="population-mean-figure" />

 Value statements are built from subjective experience. Terms will have personal meaning. For instance `income` translates to a unique value for each individual. Furthermore, an average can describe a population while obscuring the person we are trying to understand.

The same can happen with and value-laden language. Two coworkers may use `quality`, `safe`, or `done` for weeks while carrying different definitions. Each hears a familiar word and assumes shared meaning. They talk past one another until a failure.

This problem is inherent to language and AI inherits that problem at scale. The problem comes when a term underdetermines the speaker's intent and the system fills the gap. Its answer can be coherent under the inferred meaning and completely wrong for the person who asked.

### Meaningless Without Intent

Value statements need to carry the proper intent to be meaningfully applied. When talking to a person, we implicilty know the intent without having it spelled it out. Cases of communication failure largly stems from the intent not being understood.

Value statements often use familiar words that signal a judgment without explaining the intent behind it. Calling something “better,” for example, leaves important questions unanswered: better for whom, in what way, and at what cost? The table below groups words and phrases by the kinds of judgment they can express, notice the intent is still undefined.

| Kind | Examples | Implied judgment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Evaluative | `better`, `safe`, `fair`, `meaningful` | Compare against an unstated standard |
| Goal-oriented | `optimize`, `improve`, `reduce`, `protect` | Treat an outcome as desirable |
| Deontic | `must`, `should`, `permitted`, `prohibited` | Establish an obligation or boundary |
| Priority | `prefer`, `before`, `even if`, `at the expense of` | Rank competing values |
| Threshold | `at least`, `only if`, `never`, `until` | Turn a judgment into a gate |
| Affective | `painful`, `reassuring`, `alienating` | Point toward experienced consequences |
| Authority | `consent`, `deserve`, `authorized`, `accountable` | Assign standing and responsibility |


Jon's “I want something better” does not identify one goal. It can branch into several possible intents, each pointing toward different outcomes:

<Section index="04" title="Vision and Goals">

That unresolved intent is where vision begins. As AI makes average artifacts cheaper to produce, generating a plausible solution becomes less scarce. Deciding which change is worth creating—and which human consequence should govern the decision—becomes more valuable.

Imagine our business offers a book-publishing service. We could help Jon turn his teaching materials into a book, earn additional income, and reach more educators. But writing and promoting that book could also consume his evenings. Before calling it a valuable solution, we need to understand which outcomes Jon wants, how he ranks them, and what he is unwilling to sacrifice.

This is where **value statements**, **goals**, and **value propositions** connect. A value statement expresses how something matters to someone. A goal identifies a condition they want to bring about or preserve. A value proposition explains how an offer is expected to help them achieve those goals

For Jon, a possible goal might be to earn more from his expertise while protecting his family time. Our value proposition could then be:
>Turn your existing teaching materials into additional income without taking on the full workload of publishing and selling a book.

That is a proposed benefit but we haven't established fact. We still need to learn whether Jon wants it, whether we can deliver it, and whether the benefit justifies his time, effort, and expense. We also have our own goals and constraints. Can we provide that service at a cost that makes the business sustainable?

[Lena Hall captures this shift in The Signal Layer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1KOdiGgMtpY): "the differentiator is deciding what is worth building, what is worth saying, and what deserves trust. Our vision gives direction to that judgment; our value proposition makes the intended benefit explicit."

### Goals Reflect Opportunities

- A **goal** identifies a state worth bringing about or preserving.
- An **opportunity** is a condition that may enable progress toward it.
- A **solution** is an intervention expected to use that opportunity or remove an obstacle.
- An **experiment** tests whether the intervention produces the expected consequence.
- A **strategy** coordinates cognitive operations and actions over time toward a goal. It can combine inference, prediction, planning, valuation, action selection, and revision in response to feedback.

### The Causality Ladder Does Not Choose the Goal

Judea Pearl's **ladder of causation** distinguishes three increasingly powerful kinds of question:

| Rung | Activity | Question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Association | Seeing | What tends to happen when I observe `X`? |
| Intervention | Doing | What would happen if I do `X`? |
| Counterfactual | Imagining | What would have happened if I had acted differently? |

AI can assist at all three rungs when the necessary evidence and causal model are available. An agent can also generate subgoals, choose actions, and run experiments inside a delegated objective. None of that gives it volition to originate the governing goal or authority to decide which future deserves pursuit. The ladder answers increasingly powerful causal questions; it does not add a fourth rung called **purpose**.

An AI system acts because training, instructions, memory, schedules, tools, or users establish an objective and authorize action. Its instrumental autonomy can be substantial, but it still operates inside a larger system that supplies the ends, permissions, and criteria by which its actions count as progress.

The observation—“support tickets increased”—has no inherent strategic meaning. The governing goal determines what the increase represents:

- **Margin:** More tickets increase service costs, prompting questions about prevention, automation, or efficiency.
- **Retention:** More tickets may reveal product friction that could cause customers to leave.
- **Learning:** More tickets create additional evidence about unmet needs, confusing features, or emerging use cases.

Once the root goal is supplied, it becomes valuable to explore opportunities, generate solutions, design experiments, predict consequences, and compare results. That distinction separates two kinds of decision:

- **Governing decisions** establish what counts as better, whose interests matter, which time horizon matters, and which tradeoffs are legitimate.
- **Instrumental decisions** select actions expected to advance an accepted goal within supplied evidence and constraints.

“Optimize this code” can become substantially instrumental once tests, performance budgets, failure conditions, and operational constraints are specified. “Optimize my strategy” may ask the system to choose among revenue, resilience, customer welfare, employee sustainability, speed, and risk. Until those priorities are ranked, there is no single meaning of `better` waiting for the model to discover.

<Asset id="strategy-map-figure" />

Strategy also operates inside a field of goals held by other people and institutions. A company can achieve a local subgoal while undermining its governing purpose. It can hit an internal target while producing an outcome that customers, employees, partners, or regulators reject. Success is therefore relational: the question is not only whether an action worked, but whose goal it advanced and which other goals it constrained.

Accounting for relational impacts, temporal impacts, and value tradeoffs requires multiple dimensions of understanding. Do you prioritize a partner's goal above a customer's? Do you sacrifice one subgoal to stop a competitor from reaching its goal? These priorities could theoretically be detailed in a prompt. But by the time you have told the agent what you value, whose interests matter, and how those values should be ranked, you have likely already prioritized your goals.

> **Prediction alone cannot determine which goal deserves authority. Goals must be grounded in experiential change for a target audience.**

</Section>

<Section index="05" title="Authority, Accountability, and Corrigibility">

An AI can state principles, rank them, and translate them into behavior. That does not establish that it authored those principles or has authority to impose them.

Every AI system operates with an implicit value hierarchy. You can ask models to describe theirs; compare the answers from <ConversationLink model="ChatGPT" href="https://chatgpt.com/share/6a8915a6-f76c-83e8-922e-e05026381142" />, <ConversationLink model="Claude" href="https://claude.ai/share/ee135d92-4246-4424-8ff4-bfb38cfa18b6" />, and <ConversationLink model="DeepSeek" href="https://chat.deepseek.com/share/3dqzyfjd1evx1je3o6" />. Those values are implicitly shaped by:

| Source | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Training data | Associations, examples, norms, contradictions, and recurring judgments |
| Post-training | Reinforced dispositions such as helpfulness, refusal, or deference |
| System instructions | Role-specific priorities and constraints |
| Organizational policy | Delegated purpose, decision rights, and escalation |
| User context | Immediate goals, evidence, preferences, and exceptions |
| Tools and permissions | Enforceable limits on possible action |
| Evaluation and feedback | Criteria that reward, reject, or revise behavior |

These layers can agree or conflict. What the system enacts depends on how they are ordered and enforced. Implicitly or explicitly choosing the wrong values will have the same downstream consequences.

<Callout label="Key claim">

**Governed by the wrong values, the system becomes coherently wrong.**

</Callout>

<Asset id="governing-loop-figure" />

| Governing priority | Behavior rewarded | Possible consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Growth above trust | Aggressive acquisition and dark patterns | Churn, regulation, and brand erosion |
| Speed above reliability | Shipping without adequate validation | Outages and accumulated technical debt |
| Harmony above truth | Suppressing disagreement and bad news | Loss of corrective evidence |
| Metrics above purpose | Optimizing visible indicators | The measurement improves while the outcome deteriorates |
| Revenue above customer welfare | Extracting rather than creating value | Customers leave when alternatives appear |

This is <Term definition="The appearance that evaluation is complete because a system satisfies its own criteria, even though those criteria omit or misrank consequences that matter.">false evaluative closure</Term>. The system has precise criteria for calling an action better, but those criteria omit or misrank consequences that matter. Tests pass because the tests embody the wrong priorities. Dashboards remain green because the dashboards exclude the people being harmed.

AI can accelerate this failure. It can reproduce the hierarchy across more decisions, with greater speed and consistency. The model may identify a contradiction or harmful consequence, but it cannot overrule the governing system unless people have given it permission to challenge, escalate, or stop.

A resilient hierarchy must therefore be <Term definition="Answerable to evidence and authorized revision rather than protected as an untouchable objective.">corrigible</Term>: answerable to evidence and revision rather than protected as an untouchable objective. That requires:

- direct observation of customer and employee consequences
- protected disagreement and independent feedback
- perspectives from people who bear costs without controlling the decision
- measurements that include downstream effects
- explicit review of tradeoffs and uncertainty
- escalation paths with authority to revise the governing goal

Human governance does not mean manually choosing every action. It means retaining responsibility for which values govern, creating the conditions under which those values can be challenged, and changing them when their consequences reveal they were wrong.

</Section>

<Section index="06" title="From Human Judgment to Language">

Human values cannot guide an AI while remaining private. They have to become available through some combination of:

- named stakeholders and consequences
- definitions and domain distinctions
- priorities and legitimate tradeoffs
- representative examples and counterexamples
- constraints, permissions, and escalation boundaries
- evidence, provenance, and explicit uncertainty
- tests, stopping conditions, and evaluation
- feedback capable of revising the governing model

This translation does not remove the need for judgment. It makes judgment inspectable and gives both people and AI a better chance of recognizing when they are using the same words for different things.

It also opens the next question in this series. Language does not carry every kind of constraint with equal reliability. A proof, a program, an experimental report, and a product aspiration are shaped by different practices and corrective systems. In some domains an invalid interpretation is quickly rejected. In others, several incompatible interpretations can sound equally coherent.

[Truth and Inference](/writing/truth-and-inference) asks what makes the difference: how language acquires predictive structure, why code is unusually constraint-dense, and when a fluent continuation is evidence rather than merely the shape of an answer.

</Section>

<Section index="07" title="Conclusion">

The agent did not fail because it was incapable of producing a plan. It failed because `optimal` omitted the judgment that would make one plan preferable to another. The model supplied a “plausible” interpretation from its training and runtime context.

The strategic-advice study did not test this claim directly. Our hypothesis is that, had the researchers held the factual scenarios constant while explicitly authorizing different value hierarchies, the models would have returned different strategies. A company that ranks workforce continuity above near-term efficiency should not receive the same advice as one that ranks rapid transformation above continuity, even when the market facts are identical.

Therein lies the crux: an AI may infer an operative value hierarchy, propose interventions, and reason about counterfactuals, but it does not by its own volition choose the governing goal that makes those actions worth pursuing. It cannot know that the inferred hierarchy is the one you intended, and predictive competence does not grant authority to decide what you ought to value. When values remain unstated, they do not disappear. The model imports latent priorities from its training, post-training, and the language of the prompt.

</Section>
