# Vision and Values

<div className="goals-article">

<Lede>

I once gave an agent what I thought was a reasonable instruction:

<Quote>
  <P>Make this plan optimal. Find all the gaps. Add the necessary validation.</P>
</Quote>

Nine hours later, I killed the run. The agent had produced a stack of checklists,
controls, and imagined requirements because I had never said which gaps
mattered. I asked for an “optimal” plan without naming the priority, then handed
the agent authority to supply the missing judgment.

AI is making plausible output cheap to produce. In
[*The Economic Case for Generative AI*](https://a16z.com/the-economic-case-for-generative-ai/),
Martin Casado compares this shift with earlier collapses in computation and
distribution costs. Another candidate can approach
zero cost; evaluating, integrating, and operating it cannot.

<Callout label="Core thesis" emphasis>
  <P><strong>When another plausible answer becomes nearly free, judgment becomes scarce.</strong></P>
</Callout>

Which future is worth pursuing? Who decides, and what would prove us wrong?

AI can research those questions, compare options, challenge assumptions, model
consequences, and act inside a delegated objective.
None of that gives it authority to decide whose interests count or which
consequences deserve priority.

That authority remains ours because strategy commits people and institutions to
other people. For AI to act with us, that commitment must be visible,
contestable, and open to correction.

</Lede>

<Section index="01" title="A Plan Is Not a Strategy">

A strategy is not a long document. It is a set of choices made under
uncertainty: a diagnosis, a direction, accepted tradeoffs, rejected options,
and coordinated action. The document records those commitments. It cannot make
them for us.

An AI can make a document look complete before anyone has chosen what matters.
My instruction rewarded exhaustive coverage; the outcome I wanted required a
theory of which gaps could prevent success.

Research on AI strategy advice makes this harder to dismiss as one bad prompt.
Angelo Romasanta, Llewellyn Thomas, and Natalia Levina found that leading models
repeatedly favored fashionable positions across seven strategic tensions. In
their deeper ChatGPT-5 analysis, option order shifted recommendations more than
detailed company scenarios did on average. The study concerns particular
models, prompts, and tensions; it does not prove that all AI advice is wrong.
It does show why polished strategy language should not inherit authority merely
because it sounds wise.

As creation gets cheaper, the valuable work moves upstream: deciding what the
work is for.

</Section>

<Section index="02" title="Fluency Can Feel Like Understanding">

Broad advice can feel personal because we connect it to details from our own
lives and remember what fits. Psychologists call one version of this the
<Term definition="The tendency to experience broad, general descriptions as uniquely personal and accurate.">Barnum effect</Term>.
Feeling understood is not evidence that a system found the facts controlling a
decision. Perceived specificity, sycophancy, error, and deception are different
problems.

Imagine two companies asking whether to centralize product decisions. One has
six teams duplicating infrastructure and breaking shared workflows. The other
serves distinct regulated markets where local expertise matters. “Create a
unified operating model while empowering teams” sounds sensible in both cases.
It has not made the decision. The real questions are which decisions are
coupled, where knowledge lives, and which failures can remain local.

A POTS diagnosis can name a real syndrome and guide care without revealing one
underlying mechanism or explaining why a particular person developed it.
Classification is useful; it is not a complete explanation.

Strategy advice can fail the same way. Fluency names a recognizable pattern.
Strategic fit requires the distinctions that justify this choice—and the
evidence that would make us choose differently.

*This figure shows a possible failure path, not a measured rate: coherent output
can remain ungrounded in the user's actual goal.*

Some missing context is factual. Some is normative. Words such as `better`,
`safe`, `efficient`, and `valuable` do more than describe a situation. They
rank possible futures.

</Section>

<Section index="03" title="What Language Carries—and What It Leaves Out">

A language model carries extraordinary structure. Relationships among words,
concepts, and contexts let it infer, plan, explain, and use tools. But a model
of recorded language is not the same thing as acquaintance with the people the
language describes; complete coverage of recorded facts is not complete
acquaintance with a person.

It can know everything documented about Alicia yet not know Alicia is pronounced
ah-LEE-sha by her family. The pronunciation is knowable; nobody made it
available. The irritation of being repeatedly misnamed, the trust created when
someone remembers, and the history that gives the name significance are harder
to reduce to an isolated fact. Situated knowledge lives partly in the edges
between facts: relationships, history, stakes, and consequences.

<Asset id="relational-knowing-figure" />

Consider Jon. “Jon earns $72,000” describes his salary. “Jon feels underpaid”
tells us the salary does not fit what he thinks the work should provide. It does
not tell us whether he wants more income, recognition, time, or autonomy.

For this article, <Term definition="How an outcome is experienced as beneficial, harmful, meaningful, or worth protecting by a person or community.">human value</Term> means how an outcome is experienced as
beneficial, harmful, meaningful, or worth protecting. A
<Term definition="Language expressing part of how an outcome matters to a person or community.">value statement</Term> expresses part of that relationship in language. An
<Term definition="A ranking enacted in behavior or policy, whether or not anyone stated or authorized it.">operative priority</Term> is a ranking enacted in behavior or policy, whether or not anyone stated or authorized it.

A model can recite principles and behave according to learned or instructed
priorities. That does not establish that it experiences those values or has
authority to impose them. Human values concern what outcomes mean in people's
lives. Operative priorities concern what a system rewards, protects, or
sacrifices.

We do not need to transmit Jon's entire inner life before acting. We need enough
shared understanding to form a responsible hypothesis, expose the tradeoffs,
and notice when reality disagrees.

Once those meanings are on the table, we can ask the harder question: which
future should govern the work?

<Section index="04" title="Vision Gives Direction. Authority Makes It Governing.">

A <Term definition="A shared, revisable picture of a desirable future and the human consequences that make it worth pursuing.">vision</Term> is a shared, revisable picture of a desirable future and the human
consequences that make it worth pursuing. It gives direction before every goal
or plan has been fixed. But coherence, ambition, and eloquence do not make a
vision legitimate.

<Callout label="Governance test" title="Four requirements">
  <ol>
    <li><strong><Term definition="Whose interests must be considered and who has a route to contest the goal.">Standing</Term>:</strong> people affected by the goal must be considered and need a route to contest it. Standing does not give every stakeholder the same decision right or an automatic veto.</li>
    <li><strong><Term definition="Who may bind an organization to a goal and where that authority stops.">Authorization</Term>:</strong> the organization must make clear who can bind it to the goal and where that authority stops.</li>
    <li><strong><Term definition="Who remains answerable for the benefits, harms, and costs produced by pursuing the goal.">Accountability</Term>:</strong> the people who authorize and execute the goal remain responsible for the benefits and costs imposed on others.</li>
    <li><strong><Term definition="The requirement that a goal remain open to revision when evidence conflicts with its stated purpose.">Corrigibility</Term>:</strong> the goal must remain open to revision when evidence conflicts with its stated purpose.</li>
  </ol>
</Callout>

Suppose our company offers a book-publishing service. We might help Jon turn his
teaching materials into a book, reach more educators, and earn additional
income without taking on the full work of publishing. That is a promising
offer, not an authorized goal. We still need to learn whether Jon wants it,
whether family time is a governing constraint, whether we can deliver it, and
whether the benefit justifies the cost.

A goal names a condition worth creating or preserving. Once the root goal is
supplied, it becomes valuable to explore opportunities, compare solutions, and
test whether an intervention produces the intended consequence. Strategy
coordinates those commitments over time.

<Asset id="goal-tree-figure" />

*Goals determine what counts as an opportunity and how an experiment should be
judged.*

Better reasoning does not remove this dependency. Judea Pearl's ladder of
causation distinguishes association, intervention, and counterfactual questions.
AI can help at every rung when the evidence and causal model are available. The
ladder answers increasingly powerful causal questions; it does not add a fourth
rung called “what should we value?”

<Asset id="strategy-map-figure" />

*A route can be causally plausible without being authorized or worthwhile.*

A model may infer a goal from behavior or propose one from public patterns.
Fluency, prediction, and causal competence can support a decision. They do not
create standing to decide whose interests matter.

This is a normative argument, not a laboratory result. Evidence can tell us
what happened. It cannot decide, without a governing judgment, whose experience
should count or which sacrifice is legitimate.

</Section>

<Section index="05" title="A System Can Be Coherent and Still Be Wrong">

Making priorities explicit is necessary. It is not the finish line. Every
deployed AI system sits inside a hierarchy shaped by training data,
post-training, system instructions, organizational policy, tools, permissions,
and evaluation. People and institutions choose how the surrounding product
orders and enforces those influences.

In his 2026 talk
[*What's next after RLHF?*](https://ai.engineer/talks/cJ0EOzey--o-whats-next-after-rlhf),
Diogo Almeida argues that “the end game for all RLHF models is optimizing for
engagement.” His diagnosis is not a demonstrated universal objective, but it
exposes the narrower problem: people turning feedback into reward choose a
proxy, evaluators, and how to resolve disagreement. Preference is not truth,
value, authorization, or accountability.

A system can please evaluators, pass its tests, or obey its instructions while
optimizing the wrong target. Our publishing service could celebrate books,
pages, and revenue while Jon earns little after fees and loses more evenings to
work. The dashboard would report success while the value proposition failed.

<Callout label="Failure mode" title="False evaluative closure">
  <P>The system has precise criteria for calling an action better, but those criteria omit or misrank a consequence that should change the judgment. Tests pass because the tests embody the wrong priority. The dashboard stays green because it excludes the person bearing the cost.</P>
</Callout>

AI may identify that contradiction, but it cannot overrule the governing system
unless people have given it permission to challenge, escalate, or stop.

<Asset id="governing-loop-figure" />

The missing path is correction. Affected people and contrary evidence need a
way to challenge the interpretation. Someone with authority must be able to
revise the goal, metric, or boundary. That requires observing consequences,
protecting disagreement, including people who bear the costs, and maintaining
escalation paths that can change course. Without them, the loop is optimization,
not governance.

Human governance is no guarantee of wisdom. People choose bad goals, protect
status, and rationalize harm. The reason to preserve human responsibility is
not that people are always better than models. Organizations act through social
authority, and those affected need somewhere to direct consent, challenge,
blame, repair, and revision.

</Section>

<Section index="06" title="Shared Intent Lets Teams Think for Themselves">

Retaining authority over the ends does not require one leader to approve every
means. A strategy that only one person can interpret is not useful to an
organization.

Suppose product learns that Jon accepts a longer timeline if it protects his
evenings, while operations finds white-glove support unsustainable. Given only
“grow book revenue,” one team may overpromise while the other removes the help
that made the offer valuable.

Shared intent gives them a basis for different but compatible choices. The
vision is not simply “publish more books.” It is to help educators earn from
existing expertise without creating a second full-time job. Teams need the
desired future, affected people, constraints, decision rights, visible
commitments, evidence that should force reconsideration, and protected dissent.

Writing this down does not manufacture a strategy. It makes the judgment
inspectable: which premise supports an action, who is absent, and what outcome
would prove the offer failed?

Inside those boundaries, AI can research, compare explanations, generate
options, and make bounded instrumental decisions. Removing a person from each
action does not remove human responsibility for the objective, permissions,
evaluation, escalation, or consequences.

Strategic agency permits distributed action. The center need not dictate every
route; it must make the destination, boundaries, and correction process clear
enough for local judgment to remain aligned without becoming obedient.

</Section>

<Section index="07" title="Retain Authority Over the Ends">

My agent did not prove that AI cannot strategize. It revealed that `optimal`
left the governing tradeoffs open, and the generated plan could not establish
which interpretation deserved authority.

Unstated values do not disappear. A system imports priorities from its training,
instructions, tools, evaluation, and request. Stated priorities can still be
wrong. Coherence does not close the values question.

We retain strategic agency by naming the future and its worthwhile consequences;
authorizing who may govern; sharing enough intent for distributed action; and
remaining open to evidence and people who show that we got it wrong.

<Callout label="Responsibility" emphasis>
  <P><strong>The point is not to preserve a ceremonial human approval at the top of an automated system. It is to preserve a chain of responsibility from values to goals, from goals to action, and from action back to consequences.</strong></P>
</Callout>

Making priorities explicit is only the beginning. We remain responsible for
whether the goals we authorize serve the people whose lives they change.

This article sets the AI Factory's direction.

<Section index="08" title="Sources">

The argument above is my synthesis. These sources support its economic opening,
bounded examples, causal distinctions, and account of revisable valuation. They
do not independently prove the normative conclusion.

1. Martin Casado. [*The Economic Case for Generative AI*](https://a16z.com/the-economic-case-for-generative-ai/). a16z AI Revolution keynote (2023), especially the economics section beginning around 6:23. Supports the illustrative claim that generative AI can sharply reduce the marginal cost of producing another candidate artifact; it does not include evaluation, integration, operation, or judgment costs.
2. Angelo Romasanta, Llewellyn D. W. Thomas, and Natalia Levina. [“Researchers Asked LLMs for Strategic Advice. They Got ‘Trendslop’ in Return.”](https://hbr.org/2026/03/researchers-asked-llms-for-strategic-advice-they-got-trendslop-in-return) *Harvard Business Review* (2026). Supports the reported defaults, context effects, and option-order effects in the tested strategic tensions; it is not evidence that every AI recommendation is wrong.
3. Bertram R. Forer. [“The Fallacy of Personal Validation: A Classroom Demonstration of Gullibility.”](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0059240) *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* (1949). Supports the classic personal-validation effect used here as an analogy, not a claim about AI deception.
4. Pat Pataranutaporn, Yoonho Lee, Judith Amores, and Pattie Maes. [“Personal Validation Effect in LLMs.”](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/personal-validation-effect-in-llms-positive-ai-responses-bias-perceptions-of-validity-reliability-personalization-and-usefulness-of-fictitious-predictions/) CHI (2026). Supports the bounded finding that positive fictitious AI predictions received higher perceived validity, personalization, reliability, and usefulness; it does not establish that every chatbot interaction has this effect.
5. NIH Expert Consensus Meeting authors. [“Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (POTS): State of the Science and Clinical Care — Part 1.”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8455420/) (2021). Supports the diagnostic criteria and description of POTS as a heterogeneous multisystem syndrome; the article's example distinguishes diagnosis from one complete causal explanation and is not medical advice.
6. Judea Pearl. [“What Is Causal Inference?”](https://bayes.cs.ucla.edu/ijcai-july2022-bw.pdf) IJCAI (2022). Supports the ladder of association, intervention, and counterfactual reasoning; it does not select which outcome ought to govern action.
7. John Dewey. [*Theory of Valuation*](https://archive.org/details/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp) (1939). Supports a consequences-sensitive and revisable account of valuation.
8. Diogo Almeida. [*What's next after RLHF?*](https://ai.engineer/talks/cJ0EOzey--o-whats-next-after-rlhf). AI Engineer World's Fair (2026), especially 5:57–8:22. Supports Almeida's distinction between preference-optimized assistance and calibrated automation, including his engagement argument; the talk does not establish a universal objective shared by every model or prove the reliability of a later product.
