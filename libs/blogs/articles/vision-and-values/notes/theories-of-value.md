# Theories of Value

## Status and purpose

Working reference note for *Vision and Values*. This note separates
several questions that are often compressed into the word **value** and records
theories that may help the article explain the bridge between a problem, a
solution, and a worthwhile outcome.

James Lindsay's name and the existence of his February 2026 podcast proposal
have been verified. His proposal is described by its publisher as a “casual
stab,” so it should not be represented as an established or fully formalized
academic theory.

Before publication, verify claims against the primary works linked below and
prefer scholarly editions where page-accurate citation matters.

## The questions are not interchangeable

Different theories of value answer different questions:

| Domain | Question |
| --- | --- |
| **Production** | What social activity or resources constitute the value of a commodity? |
| **Exchange and price** | Why does a good exchange at a particular rate or price? |
| **Subjective importance** | Why does this person value this good under these conditions? |
| **Instrumental value** | What end does the thing help achieve? |
| **Value in use** | What value is realized when a beneficiary uses a good or service in context? |
| **Prudential value** | What makes a person's life go better for that person? |
| **Normative value** | Which ends are worth pursuing, and what reasons justify them? |

The theories below are therefore not always competitors. Marx's theory of
commodity value and a theory of human well-being can both be true or false
independently because they describe different relations.

## A working vocabulary

### Use, exchange, and price

- **Use-value:** the usefulness of something or its capacity to satisfy a need.
- **Exchange-value:** what a commodity commands relative to another commodity;
  in Marx, this is a form in which value appears rather than a synonym for
  usefulness.
- **Price:** a monetary exchange ratio. A theory explaining price need not
  establish what is morally or intrinsically valuable.

### Instrumental, intrinsic, and final value

- **Instrumental value:** something is valuable as a means to another end. A
  tool, medicine, or candidate solution is commonly valuable in this sense.
- **Final value:** something is valued as an end or for its own sake.
- **Intrinsic value:** value something has in virtue of its intrinsic
  properties. Philosophers debate whether intrinsic and final value coincide.

The distinction is essential to the article:

> A solution's problem-solving capacity gives it instrumental value. It does
> not, by itself, explain why the solved condition is finally, prudentially, or
> normatively valuable.

See the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries on
[*Value Theory*](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2024/entries/value-theory/)
and
[*Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Value*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/value-intrinsic-extrinsic/).

## 1. Marx's theory of commodity value

### Central question

How is value constituted and regulated in a capitalist system of commodity
production, and how can labor-power become a source of surplus value?

### Core account

Marx distinguishes use-value, value, and exchange-value.

- A commodity must be useful to someone. Useless labor does not create value
  merely because effort occurred.
- The **substance** of commodity value is abstract human labor.
- The **magnitude** of value is regulated by the labor time socially necessary
  to produce the commodity under normal conditions and prevailing productivity,
  not by the time an unusually slow individual happened to spend.
- Exchange-value expresses the value of one commodity relative to another.
- Money supplies a general value form, but market price may diverge from value.

Marx is not merely proposing a psychology of why consumers want things. His
subject is a historically specific social relation in which privately organized
labor is made commensurable through commodity exchange. Commodity fetishism is
the appearance that value is a natural property of things rather than a form of
social relation among producers.

### Surplus value

Labor-power is itself sold as a commodity. Its value is related to the socially
necessary labor required to reproduce it, while its use can produce more value
than that reproduction requires. The difference is the basis of surplus value
in Marx's account.

### What the theory explains well

- Marx's critique of capitalist commodity production.
- The relation among abstract labor, productivity, value, and exploitation.
- Why value should not be treated as a timeless physical property embedded in
  an object.

### What it is not designed to explain by itself

- The complete phenomenology of why one person cares about an outcome.
- Moral value or a theory of human flourishing.
- The exact price of every scarce, unique, financial, or non-reproducible good.

## 2. James Lindsay's problem-solving theory of value

### Central question

What makes a good, commodity, or service valuable to the people who might use
or consume it?

### Core proposal

The official description of James Lindsay's February 13, 2026 podcast episode
defines the proposal this way: value is determined in relation to a good,
commodity, or service's capacity to solve problems for people, particularly its
consumers.

The following expression is an editorial reconstruction, not a formula
published by Lindsay:

$$
V_P(g,c)
=f\bigl(
I(p,c),
E(g,p,c)
\bigr)
$$

where:

- $g$ is a good, service, or candidate solution;
- $c$ is a consumer or beneficiary;
- $p$ is a problem;
- $I(p,c)$ is the importance of the problem to that person; and
- $E(g,p,c)$ is the effectiveness of the good in resolving it.

This reconstruction makes explicit two variables that the short official
description leaves compressed: problem importance and solution effectiveness.

### Strengths

- Directs attention away from effort and toward achieved outcomes.
- Makes value relational: a solution is valuable *to someone* and *for
  something*.
- Fits product, engineering, entrepreneurship, and service language.
- Encourages empirical testing of whether a solution changes the relevant
  condition.

### Open problems

- A condition does not identify itself as a problem; someone must interpret it.
- Solving a stated problem does not establish that the desired outcome is good.
- The proposal does not yet explain price, scarcity, substitutes, opportunity
  cost, distribution, externalities, power, or who bears a solution's harms.
- A problem can be manufactured, misdiagnosed, trivial, or important only
  because of a destructive goal.
- The same solution can have different or opposing value for different people.

### Relationship to Marx

Lindsay presents the proposal in opposition to the labor theory of value, but
the two accounts do not cleanly answer the same question. Problem-solving
capacity resembles **use-value** or consumer-relative usefulness. Marx's value
theory analyzes the social form and magnitude of commodity value in capitalist
production. A good could solve a problem and possess use-value while its
exchange relations are analyzed through an entirely different theory.

Lindsay's episode description also simplifies Marx when it says that labor is
what makes something valuable. Marx explicitly denies that useless labor
creates value and uses socially necessary abstract labor rather than individual
effort as the relevant measure.

### Source

- James Lindsay,
  [“Introducing the Problem-Solving Theory of Value,” *New Discourses Podcast*, episode 192](https://newdiscourses.com/2026/02/introducing-problem-solving-theory-value/),
  February 13, 2026

## 3. Menger's subjective and marginal theory of value

### Central question

Why does a particular quantity of a good have economic importance to a person?

### Core account

Carl Menger argues that value is not an inherent property of a good. Goods gain
value when people understand that satisfying some need depends on their command
of those goods. Available quantity matters: the value of a unit reflects the
importance of the least important satisfaction that would be lost without that
unit—the foundation of marginal utility.

This helps explain the water–diamond pattern. Water can be vastly more useful
in general while an additional unit is cheap where water is abundant. A unit of
a scarce good can command much more because a comparatively important
satisfaction depends on it.

### Relationship to Lindsay

Menger is Lindsay's closest established economic relative:

```text
Lindsay: value comes from solving a person's problem
Menger: value comes from need satisfaction that depends on command of a scarce unit
```

Menger supplies several elements missing from the short problem-solving
proposal: subjectivity, scarcity, available quantity, alternative uses, and
marginal importance. Neither account alone establishes whether the person's
need or desired outcome is morally worth satisfying.

### Primary source

- Carl Menger,
  [*Principles of Economics*, Chapter III: “The Theory of Value”](https://newaustrianeconomics.com/library/principles-of-economics/chapter-iii)

## 4. Marshall's supply-and-demand synthesis

### Central question

How do demand-side utility and supply-side production conditions jointly shape
market value and price?

### Core account

Alfred Marshall rejected the demand for a single universal blade of value. His
famous scissors analogy says that debating whether utility or production cost
governs value is like debating which blade of scissors cuts the paper. Demand,
marginal utility, supply, and costs operate together, with their relative
importance changing across time horizons.

### Relevance to the article

A product's capacity to solve a problem can influence demand. It still does not
determine market price alone. Price also depends on alternatives, scarcity,
production capacity, costs, competition, bargaining, and institutions.

### Primary source

- Alfred Marshall,
  [*Principles of Economics*, Book V, Chapter III](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/marshall/bk5ch03.htm)

## 5. Dewey's pragmatist theory of valuation

### Central question

How do people form, test, and revise value judgments when action is blocked or
a situation becomes problematic?

### Core account

John Dewey rejects a rigid separation between fixed ends and merely technical
means. A problematic situation occasions inquiry. The agent formulates an
**end-in-view**—a provisional description of a satisfactory resolution—then
tests actions and revises both means and ends in light of consequences.

Valuation is therefore not simply attaching a score to a predetermined goal.
It is part of intelligent problem formation and experimental action. Better
understanding the problem can change what counts as a solution; observing the
solution can change what the agent values.

### Relationship to Lindsay

Dewey provides a deeper problem-solving account of value because he treats the
problem and preferred outcome as revisable:

```text
problematic situation
→ proposed end-in-view
→ candidate means
→ action and consequences
→ revised problem and end
```

This is particularly important for AI. A system can optimize a supplied
objective without participating in the lived disruption that made the
situation problematic. It can also recommend a solution without possessing the
authority or evidence required to revise the governing end.

## 6. Service-dominant logic and value-in-use

### Central question

Where and by whom is value created when goods and services are used?

### Core account

Service-dominant logic, associated with Stephen Vargo and Robert Lusch, shifts
attention from value embedded in products to value realized in use and context.
Firms offer resources and value propositions; value is co-created among actors
and includes the beneficiary's integration of those resources into a lived
situation.

The later formulation holds that value is uniquely and phenomenologically
determined by the beneficiary. This does not necessarily mean phenomenal
consciousness in the philosophy-of-mind sense; in this marketing literature it
emphasizes the beneficiary's contextual experience and judgment.

### Relationship to Lindsay

This is close to problem-solving value but adds three useful corrections:

1. a producer cannot unilaterally declare that value has been delivered;
2. value is realized in context rather than contained in the artifact; and
3. multiple actors and resources participate in creating the outcome.

For the article, AI output is better described as a **value proposition** or
candidate resource until a person or system integrates it into action and the
consequences are observed.

### Sources

- Stephen L. Vargo and Robert F. Lusch,
  [“Evolving to a New Dominant Logic for Marketing”](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.68.1.1.24036)
  (2004)
- Stephen L. Vargo and Robert F. Lusch,
  [“Service-Dominant Logic: Continuing the Evolution”](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-007-0069-6)
  (2008)

## 7. Sen's capability approach

### Central question

What should count as an improvement in human welfare or development?

### Core account

Amartya Sen's capability approach evaluates people's substantive opportunities
to do and be things they have reason to value. It distinguishes resources from
what people can actually achieve with them. Personal, social, political, and
environmental conversion factors affect whether the same resource becomes a
real capability for different people.

This avoids reducing welfare to income, resource possession, pleasure, or
preference satisfaction alone. A formally available solution may lack value if
the affected person cannot convert it into a meaningful functioning.

### Relationship to problem solving

A solution can be evaluated by whether it expands or protects substantive
capability:

- Can the person actually do something they could not reliably do before?
- Does the solution expand agency or merely alter a metric?
- Are the enabling conditions present for this particular person?
- Does the intervention remove one obstacle while creating another?

The capability approach still requires normative selection: not every possible
capability is valuable, and societies must reason about which capabilities
matter.

### Sources

- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy,
  [“The Capability Approach”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/)
- Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999)

## 8. Theories of prudential value and well-being

These theories ask what is ultimately **good for a person**. They can supply the
terminal-value account that a problem-solving theory lacks.

### Hedonism

Well-being consists in a favorable balance of pleasure over pain. Its advantage
is a direct connection to felt experience. Its difficulty is whether pleasure
alone captures knowledge, authenticity, accomplishment, relationship, or
agency.

### Desire-satisfaction theories

Well-being consists in fulfillment of a person's desires or preferences. This
fits subjective and economic models but faces malformed, uninformed,
manipulated, self-destructive, and adaptive preferences.

### Objective-list theories

Some goods contribute to well-being independently of immediate pleasure or
actual desire. Candidate goods include knowledge, friendship, health,
achievement, autonomy, and meaningful activity. The challenge is justifying the
list without paternalism or arbitrary intuition.

### Hybrid theories

Some accounts require both objective goods and an appropriate subjective
relationship to them. Friendship or accomplishment might benefit someone only
when it also engages their desires, enjoyment, identification, or endorsement.

### Source

- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy,
  [“Well-Being”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/well-being/)

## 9. Fitting-attitude and buck-passing theories

### Central question

What does it mean to call something valuable?

### Core account

Fitting-attitude theories connect value to appropriate responses: something is
good when it is fitting or correct to desire, admire, protect, prefer, or
otherwise respond positively to it.

T. M. Scanlon's buck-passing formulation says, roughly, that calling something
valuable points to other properties of it that give us reasons to respond in
certain ways. Value is not an unexplained additional property; the explanatory
work is passed to the reasons supplied by the object's other features.

### Relevance to problem solving

This supplies a normative challenge to a simple solution theory:

> What properties of the problem, outcome, affected people, and consequences
> give us reasons to pursue this solution?

An AI can enumerate and test reasons under a supplied framework. The presence
of a high-scoring objective does not itself prove that the objective supplies
good reasons or that the system has authority to settle disagreement among
them.

### Source

- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy,
  [“Fitting Attitude Theories of Value”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fitting-attitude-theories/)

## A combined value stack for the article

No single theory needs to do every job. The article can trace distinct layers:

| Layer | Governing question | Useful theory |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Condition** | What is happening? | Empirical observation and causal inquiry |
| **Problem** | Why is this condition unsatisfactory, and to whom? | Deweyan valuation, lived experience, reasons |
| **Outcome** | What would count as better? | Well-being, capabilities, normative theory |
| **Solution** | What intervention can cause the change? | Lindsay's problem-solving value, instrumental value |
| **Use** | Is value actually realized in context? | Service-dominant logic, value-in-use |
| **Choice** | How important is this solution relative to alternatives and scarcity? | Menger, marginal utility, decision theory |
| **Price** | What exchange ratio emerges? | Marshallian supply and demand, institutions and bargaining |
| **Production** | How is commodity value socially constituted and surplus produced? | Marxian value theory |
| **Revision** | Did the consequences justify the original problem and goal? | Deweyan inquiry and accountable evaluation |

The article's central bridge can then be stated precisely:

> **A solution is instrumentally valuable when it reliably changes a condition
> toward a desired outcome. Its total value remains underdetermined until we
> identify whose problem is being solved, why the outcome is worth pursuing,
> how value is realized in context, which harms and alternatives matter, and who
> is responsible for revising the judgment after consequences appear.**

## Implications for AI

An AI system can participate in every layer, but participation should not be
confused with ownership:

- It can detect patterns without experiencing the condition as a problem.
- It can represent goals without originating their normative authority.
- It can generate solutions without determining that the outcome is good.
- It can predict consequences without bearing them.
- It can optimize a metric without knowing whether the metric remains a valid
  proxy for human value.
- It can help revise a judgment when feedback is supplied without independently
  maintaining the institutional responsibility to seek that feedback.

This does not prove that artificial systems can never possess their own values.
It gives a bounded operational rule for present systems:

> Locate the person or system that supplies the problem judgment, goal,
> persistence, evaluative criteria, and responsibility before attributing the
> value of the solution to the model.

## Candidate claims for the article

- “A solution is never merely an answer; it is an intervention judged relative
  to a valued outcome.”
- “Problem-solving explains instrumental value, not why the problem matters.”
- “Labor, utility, price, usefulness, well-being, and moral worth are not six
  measurements of one hidden substance. They describe different relations.”
- “An AI can optimize the bridge between a condition and a goal without being
  the source of the goal.”
- “A value proposition becomes value only when it survives use, consequences,
  and revision.”
- “The ability to solve a problem does not include the authority to decide
  which problems should exist.”
