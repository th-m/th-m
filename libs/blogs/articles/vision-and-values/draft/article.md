# Vision and Values

I once gave an agent what I thought was a clever instruction:

> Optimize this plan. Find all the gaps. Add the necessary validation.

Nine hours later, I killed the run. The agent had produced pages of checklists,
controls, and imagined product requirements. It had found gaps everywhere
because I had never said which gaps mattered. It had optimized the plan without
knowing what *optimal* meant.

The failure was not a shortage of intelligence or effort. The agent generated
far more possibilities than I could have generated in the same time. The
problem was direction. I had delegated the word `better` without supplying the
judgment that would make one outcome better than another.

That distinction becomes more important as generation becomes cheaper. In
[*The Economic Case for Generative AI*](https://a16z.com/the-economic-case-for-generative-ai/),
Martin Casado compares generative AI with earlier shifts that drove down the
marginal cost of computation and distribution. His image-generation example
suggests that the cost of producing another candidate artifact can approach
zero. “Zero” is a direction, not a literal accounting claim. Inference,
evaluation, integration, and operation still cost money and attention. Finished
work is not free.

But when another plausible plan, image, or page of code becomes nearly free,
production stops being the only scarce thing. Judgment becomes scarce: Which
problem matters? Which future is worth pursuing? Which sacrifices are
acceptable? Who gets to decide? What evidence should make us change course?

This article is about retaining authority over those questions. AI can research,
compare, predict, challenge, and act within a delegated objective. It can even
propose objectives. None of those capabilities gives it legitimate authority to
decide whose interests matter or which consequences deserve priority.

Strategic agency does not require people to choose every action. It requires us
to authorize the governing values, make them visible enough to guide action,
and keep them answerable to the people and consequences they affect.

## 1. When Creation Becomes Cheap, Judgment Becomes Scarce

Strategy is not the production of a large plan. It is a set of commitments under
uncertainty. A strategy diagnoses a situation, chooses what to pursue, accepts
some tradeoffs, rejects others, and coordinates action over time. The choices
are the strategy. A document records them.

An AI system can make a strategy document look complete before those choices
have been made. That is what happened in my nine-hour run. The agent interpreted
`find all the gaps` as a request for exhaustive coverage. I had meant something
closer to: identify the gaps most likely to prevent the outcome, especially at
the boundaries where one part of the plan depends on another. Those are very
different instructions. The first rewards volume. The second requires a theory
of what matters.

Research on AI strategy advice makes the danger more concrete. Angelo
Romasanta, Llewellyn Thomas, and Natalia Levina tested leading models across
seven strategic tensions. They report that the models repeatedly favored
fashionable positions despite changes in organizational context. In their
deeper ChatGPT-5 analysis, asking for more reasoning or changing the framing
barely shifted two of the favored positions; richer company scenarios shifted
responses more, while reversing the order of the options shifted them more
still.

Those findings are bounded to the models, prompts, and strategic tensions the
authors tested. They do not prove that every AI recommendation is wrong or that
AI-assisted companies lose. They do show why a polished recommendation should
not inherit authority merely because it sounds strategic. The response may
reflect patterns in public management language, the order of the choices, or
the priorities built into the surrounding system more than the distinction that
actually matters in this organization.

When production is abundant, the valuable act moves upstream: deciding what the
work is for.

## 2. Fluency Can Impersonate Fit

Generic advice often feels more specific than it is because the recipient does
part of the interpretive work. We connect broad language to private details,
remember the lines that fit, and supply distinctions the advice never earned.
Psychologists call one version of this the Barnum effect: broadly applicable
statements can feel personally diagnostic.

This does not mean every agreeable answer is generic, every generic answer is
false, or every mistake is deception. Perceived specificity, sycophancy,
factual error, and deliberate deception are different problems. The practical
point is simpler: feeling understood is not evidence that the system has
identified the facts that discriminate among choices.

Suppose two companies ask whether they should centralize product decisions. One
has six teams duplicating the same infrastructure and breaking shared customer
workflows. The other serves distinct regulated markets whose local teams hold
the relevant expertise. “Create a unified operating model while empowering
teams” sounds appropriate in both cases. It has not made the decision. The
decisive questions concern which decisions are coupled, where the knowledge
lives, which failures can be contained, and which commitments must remain
shared.

A medical analogy makes the difference between naming and explaining especially
clear. Imagine a woman who seeks help for dizziness, fatigue, and
lightheadedness when standing. Orthostatic testing supports a diagnosis of
postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, or POTS. That diagnosis names a real,
measurable syndrome and can guide care. It does not, by itself, identify one
underlying mechanism, explain why she developed it, or supply a cure. If a
clinician presents the label as the complete answer to *why*, classification has
performed the role of explanation without providing one.

Strategy advice can fail in the same way. Domain fluency can name a pattern
before it establishes why that pattern matters here. The test is not whether the
answer sounds like expert language. The test is whether it identifies the
distinctions that would justify this choice and the evidence that would reverse
it.

## 3. Values Begin in Lived Stakes

Consider a person named Jon. The statement “Jon earns $72,000” describes his
salary. “Jon feels underpaid” tells us that his salary does not fit his sense of
what his work should provide. The second statement carries value, but it still
does not tell us what Jon wants to change.

Does he want more income? More recognition? Less work? Better benefits? A path
to ownership? Would he accept less family time for higher pay? Would he trade
salary for autonomy? Each answer points toward a different future.

For this article, I will use **human value** to mean how an outcome is
experienced as beneficial, harmful, meaningful, or worth protecting by a person
or community. A **value statement** expresses some part of that relationship in
language. An **operative priority** is different: it is a ranking that a person,
organization, or system enacts, whether or not anyone stated or authorized it.

That distinction matters when we say that an AI system has “values.” A model can
state principles and behave according to learned or instructed priorities. That
does not establish that it experiences those values or has authority to impose
them. Human values concern what outcomes mean in people's lives. Operative
priorities concern what the system actually rewards, protects, or sacrifices.

**[ASSET: relational-knowing-figure]**

*Facts become strategically meaningful through relationships, history, stakes,
and consequences. A complete record of isolated facts is not complete
acquaintance with the person affected by them.*

Language lets private meaning travel, but the translation is never complete.
Two coworkers can use `quality`, `safe`, or `done` for weeks while carrying
different thresholds and reference points. Each hears a familiar word and
assumes shared meaning. The disagreement becomes visible only when a decision
forces the hidden definitions apart.

This is not an argument that language is useless. We do not need to transmit
Jon's entire inner life before acting. We need enough shared understanding to
form a responsible hypothesis, expose the tradeoffs, and recognize evidence
that we are wrong.

*Personal meaning is not yet shared value. Experience can carry meaning before

The two missing bridges in the graph are practical. First, meaning must become
communicable enough for other people to understand and challenge it. Second, a
claimed benefit must be tested in the relationships where it is supposed to

summary can all be important inputs. None closes the question alone.

## 4. Vision Organizes Goals; Authority Makes Them Governing

A **vision** is a shared, revisable picture of a desirable future and the human
consequences that make it worth pursuing. It provides direction before every
goal or plan has been fixed. A vision is not legitimate merely because it is
coherent or inspiring. It has to be connected to people with standing, a reason
the organization may act, and a process for learning whether the promised
future is actually desirable.

Imagine that our company offers a book-publishing service. We could help Jon
turn his teaching materials into a book, reach more educators, and earn income
from expertise he already has. We might propose this value proposition:

> Turn your existing teaching materials into additional income without taking
> on the full workload of publishing and selling a book.

That is a useful proposal. It is not yet a fact. We still need to learn whether
Jon wants the outcome, whether protecting family time is truly a governing
constraint, whether we can deliver the service, and whether the benefit
justifies the cost.

The distinctions are straightforward:

- A **goal** identifies a condition worth bringing about or preserving.
- An **opportunity** is a condition that may enable progress toward that goal.
- A **solution** is an intervention expected to use the opportunity or remove
  an obstacle.
- An **experiment** tests whether the intervention produces the expected
  consequence.
- A **strategy** coordinates commitments and actions toward the goal under
  uncertainty.

Goals determine what counts as an opportunity. The existence of a new model,
market, or technology does not identify its own valuable use.

**[ASSET: goal-tree-figure]**

*A governing goal gives opportunities, solutions, and experiments their
direction. The tree should distinguish a proposed goal from one that has been
authorized to guide action.*

More capable reasoning does not remove this dependency. Judea Pearl's ladder of
causation distinguishes three kinds of question:

| Rung | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Association | What tends to happen when I observe `X`? |
| Intervention | What would happen if I do `X`? |
| Counterfactual | What would have happened if I had acted differently? |

AI can assist at all three rungs when the necessary evidence and causal model
are available. An agent can generate subgoals, compare interventions, run
experiments, and revise a plan inside a delegated objective. The ladder can help
us choose a route. It does not decide which destination deserves pursuit.

Consider an increase in support tickets. Under a margin goal, it may represent
cost and prompt automation. Under a retention goal, it may reveal product
friction. Under a learning goal, it may be valuable evidence of unmet needs.
The observation is the same. The governing goal determines what the observation
means for action.

**[ASSET: strategy-map-figure]**

*Prediction and causal reasoning compare possible routes. Values determine
which effects count as desirable, whose costs count, and which route may be
authorized.*

An AI system may infer a goal from behavior or propose one from public patterns.
That is not the same as authorizing it. Prediction, fluency, and causal
competence can support a decision, but they do not create standing to decide
whose interests matter.

My claim is that a governing goal needs four things beyond plausibility:

1. **Standing.** People affected by the goal must be considered and must have
   routes to contest it. Standing does not give every stakeholder the same
   decision right or an automatic veto.
2. **Authorization.** The organization must make explicit who may bind it to the
   goal and where that authority ends.
3. **Accountability.** The people who authorize and execute the goal remain
   responsible for the benefits and costs imposed on others.
4. **Corrigibility.** The goal must be open to challenge and revision when
   evidence shows that it conflicts with its stated purpose.

This is a normative argument, not a laboratory finding. Causal evidence can
show what happened. It cannot, without a governing judgment, decide whose
experience should count or which sacrifice is legitimate.

## 5. A Coherent System Can Still Be Wrong

Making priorities explicit is necessary. It is not sufficient.

Every AI system operates inside an effective hierarchy of priorities. The
hierarchy comes from several places:

| Source | What it contributes |
| --- | --- |
| Training data | Patterns, examples, norms, contradictions, and recurring judgments |
| Post-training | Reinforced dispositions such as helpfulness, refusal, confidence, or deference |
| System instructions | Role-specific priorities and constraints |
| Organizational policy | Delegated purpose, decision rights, and escalation rules |
| User context | Immediate goals, evidence, preferences, and exceptions |
| Tools and permissions | Enforceable limits on possible action |
| Evaluation and feedback | Criteria that reward, reject, or revise behavior |

These layers can agree or conflict. What the system enacts depends on how they
are ordered and enforced.

In his 2026 talk
[*What's next after RLHF?*](https://ai.engineer/talks/cJ0EOzey--o-whats-next-after-rlhf),
Diogo Almeida argues that “the end game for all RLHF models is optimizing for
engagement.” That is his diagnosis of preference-training incentives, not a
demonstrated universal objective inside every model or deployment. The narrower
lesson is enough: turning human feedback into a reward signal does not settle
the values question. It selects a proxy, a population of evaluators, and a
procedure for resolving disagreement.

Human preference is not identical to truth, value, authorization, or
accountability. A system trained to please evaluators may become more usable
while becoming less willing to expose uncertainty or disagreement. A system
trained for correctness may still optimize the wrong measurable target. A
system that obeys its instructions perfectly may faithfully reproduce a harmful
priority.

Return to Jon. Our publishing service could celebrate books released, pages
produced, and revenue collected while Jon earns little after fees and spends
more evenings working. The dashboard would report success while the value
proposition failed.

This is **false evaluative closure**: the system has precise criteria for
calling an action better, but those criteria omit or misrank a consequence that
should change the judgment. Tests pass because the tests embody the wrong
priority. Metrics rise because the dashboard excludes the person bearing the
cost.

AI can accelerate this failure. It can reproduce the hierarchy across more
decisions, faster and more consistently. The model may identify a contradiction
or harmful consequence, but it cannot overrule the governing system unless
people have given it permission to challenge, escalate, or stop.

**[ASSET: governing-loop-figure]**

*The current figure shows the failure loop: governing values become metrics and
incentives, repeated local decisions produce consequences, and filtered data
appears to confirm the original hierarchy.*

The figure earns its place by showing why internal coherence can become a trap.
The correction path is what the closed loop lacks: affected people and contrary
evidence must be able to challenge the interpretation, and someone with
authority must be able to revise the goal, metric, or boundary. Without that
path, the loop is optimization, not governance.

A resilient hierarchy therefore needs:

- direct observation of customer, employee, and partner consequences;
- protected disagreement and independent feedback;
- perspectives from people who bear costs without controlling the decision;
- measurements that include downstream effects and failure conditions;
- explicit review of tradeoffs and uncertainty; and
- escalation paths with authority to revise or stop the governing objective.

Human governance does not mean human infallibility. People authorize bad goals,
ignore evidence, protect status, and rationalize harm. The reason to preserve
human responsibility is not that people are always wiser than models. It is
that organizations act through social authority, and the people affected by
those actions need somewhere to direct consent, challenge, blame, repair, and
revision.

## 6. Shared Intent Makes Local Judgment Possible

Retaining authority over the ends does not require one leader to approve every
means. In fact, a strategy that only one person can interpret is not very useful
to an organization.

Suppose two teams work on the publishing service. The product team learns that
Jon would accept a longer publishing timeline if the service protects his
evenings. The operations team learns that full white-glove support makes the
business unsustainable. If both teams only receive the instruction “grow book
revenue,” they may optimize against each other. Product may add commitments
operations cannot support; operations may remove the very assistance that made
the offer valuable.

Shared intent gives them a basis for different but compatible decisions. The
vision is not “publish more books.” It is to help educators earn from existing
expertise without imposing a second full-time job. Product can reduce the work
required from authors. Operations can standardize the parts that do not require
personal judgment. Both can challenge the strategy if the economics make the
promise impossible.

Teams need enough common context to exercise that judgment:

- the desired future and the people whose lives should improve;
- the diagnosis and reasons for pursuing it;
- governing priorities, non-goals, and unacceptable tradeoffs;
- causal assumptions connecting action to expected benefit;
- local decision rights and commitments that affect other teams;
- evidence and consequences that would challenge the direction; and
- protected routes to escalate, dissent, and revise.

This is not a template for manufacturing strategy. Filling in the boxes does not
supply the distinctions, commitments, or legitimacy. It makes the current
judgment inspectable. People and AI can then ask better questions: Which premise
supports this action? Which stakeholder is absent? Which tradeoff have we hidden?
What outcome would prove that the offer failed?

Within those boundaries, AI can do substantial work. It can research the
situation, compare rival explanations, generate options, simulate
counterarguments, identify inconsistencies, and make bounded instrumental
decisions. Removing a person from each individual action does not remove human
responsibility for the objective, evaluator, permissions, escalation policy, or
consequences.

Strategic agency is therefore compatible with distributed action. The center
does not need to dictate every route. It needs to make the destination,
boundaries, and correction process clear enough that local judgment can remain
aligned without becoming obedient.

## 7. Retain Authority Over the Ends, Not Every Action

My agent did not prove that AI cannot strategize. It proved something narrower
about my request: `optimal` left the governing tradeoffs open, and the generated
plan could not establish which interpretation deserved authority.

That is the recurring danger of abundant, fluent output. When values remain
unstated, they do not disappear. The system imports operative priorities from
its training, post-training, instructions, tools, evaluation, and the language
of the request. When priorities are stated, they can still be incomplete or
wrong. Coherence does not close the values question.

People and organizations retain strategic agency by doing four things:

1. Name the future they are trying to create and the human consequences that
   make it worth pursuing.
2. Authorize who may make governing and instrumental decisions, with explicit
   boundaries.
3. Share enough intent that teams and systems can act without waiting for one
   central interpreter.
4. Keep the governing hierarchy contestable and corrigible when evidence or
   affected people show that it is wrong.

The point is not to protect a ceremonial human decision at the top of an
automated system. The point is to preserve a chain of responsibility from
values to goals, from goals to action, and from action back to consequences.

Making priorities explicit is the beginning, not the end. We remain responsible
for whether the goals we authorize—and the systems that pursue them—actually
serve the people whose lives they change.
