# Graphical Interfaces for Music Composition: A Research Map

## Research conclusion

The attached image is **not an mDecks page**. Its footer identifies it as *Illustrated Harmony*, and Brian Calli’s product description confirms the method: chord symbols are connected by arrows in colorful diagrams, deliberately avoiding conventional staff notation so that harmony can be explored as a network of possible paths. Calli describes it as a visual, creative rather than academic guide to harmonic exploration. citeturn19view1

![The attached reference: a directed harmonic network from Illustrated Harmony](sandbox:/mnt/data/major%20and%20minor.png)

mDecks is pursuing a closely related idea from a more traditional functional-harmony direction. Its **Mapping Tonal Harmony** system places tonal functions and cadences around the circle of fifths, with stable spatial locations for functions such as V7 and IV and regions for tonic, subdominant, and dominant. The current mDecks catalog sells a Mapping Tonal Harmony PDF alongside composition, reharmonization, jazz-progression, voicing, and other interactive books. citeturn19view2turn20view0turn20view2

The important finding is that **“graphical music composition” is not one interface category**. There are at least about **eighteen fundamentally different representation paradigms**. They encode different things: notes, chords, voice-leading distance, harmonic function, rhythm, formal structure, transformations, algorithms, signal flow, timbre, probability, or performer freedom.

The closest precedents to the interface in your image are, in descending conceptual similarity:

**directed chord networks → Nodal-style traversable graphs → Tonnetz/neo-Riemannian lattices → mDecks functional maps → Hookpad's contextual chord/melody workspace → geometric chord spaces.**

That cluster looks especially promising if the goal is a composition interface that answers **“where can the music go next?”** rather than merely **“what notes have I already entered?”**

## The full design space

The following is an exhaustive taxonomy at the **representation-paradigm level**. Individual implementations number in the hundreds, but almost all graphical composition systems I found reduce to one or a hybrid of these models.

| Graphical paradigm | What the picture means | Composition interaction | Representative systems / literature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Staff / symbolic score** | Vertical position = pitch; horizontal ordering = musical time; glyphs encode duration, articulation, dynamics, meter, etc. | Enter/edit fully specified musical events | MuseScore Studio, Flat, Dorico, StaffPad. MuseScore describes itself as professional notation software; StaffPad converts handwritten notation into editable typeset notation. citeturn26search0turn26search14turn24search0 |
| **Handwritten score** | Same musical model as staff notation, but pen gestures are the interface | Compose almost as on paper, then let software interpret it | StaffPad is the clearest production implementation: handwriting, touch editing, audio and playback. citeturn24search0turn24search4 |
| **Piano roll / pitch-time plane** | X = time, Y = pitch, rectangle length = duration | Draw, stretch and move notes directly | FL Studio is an archetypal implementation; its Piano Roll sends note and automation information to instruments. citeturn23search0 |
| **Step-sequencer grid** | Columns = discrete time steps; rows = drums, notes or parameters | Toggle cells/pulses on and off | This is one of the dominant beat-oriented representations inside modern DAWs; FL Studio combines step sequencing with piano-roll and arrangement views. citeturn16search1turn23search0 |
| **Linear arrangement / block timeline** | X = global song time; blocks = clips, phrases or recordings | Move/duplicate whole musical sections | Ableton calls this its **Arrangement View**, where events occur along a fixed song timeline. citeturn23search1 |
| **Clip / scene matrix** | One axis = tracks; other = interchangeable clips/scenes rather than absolute time | Trigger and recombine formal units non-linearly | Ableton **Session View** explicitly contrasts with the fixed timeline and is used for experimenting and combining ideas. citeturn23search1 |
| **Tracker / event table** | Time runs vertically through rows; columns encode notes, instruments, effects and parameters | Type events numerically/keyboard-first | Renoise is the modern high-profile example of the vertical tracker paradigm. citeturn24search1turn24search9 |
| **Chord-chart / harmonic timeline** | Cells or bars encode chord symbols rather than every note | Compose harmonic rhythm/form at a high level | iReal Pro centers writing and sharing chord charts; Hookpad similarly separates harmonic/melodic structure from low-level production. iReal Pro reports more than 2 million musicians worldwide. citeturn13search5turn19view3 |
| **Circle-of-fifths / functional map** | Spatial direction encodes key proximity and harmonic function | Navigate tonal relationships rather than a timeline | **mDecks Mapping Tonal Harmony** organizes diatonic functions and cadences around the circle of fifths into tonic/subdominant/dominant regions. citeturn19view2 |
| **Directed chord-progression graph** | Nodes = chords; arrows = available or meaningful transitions | Follow, branch, backtrack or discover harmonic paths | **Illustrated Harmony** is the example in your image: chord symbols and arrows are intentionally substituted for conventional notation. citeturn19view1 |
| **Traversable musical graph** | Nodes = musical events; edges = temporal/structural possibilities | Agents move through the graph to generate a piece | **Nodal** is unusually close to the abstract idea: user-defined nodes contain events, edges connect them, and virtual players traverse the network; edge length can determine travel time. citeturn24search26 |
| **Tonnetz / harmonic lattice** | Pitches occupy a repeating geometric lattice; nearby triangles/regions represent harmonically close triads | Move spatially between chords through shared tones and transformations | Tonnetz is used for transformational harmonic analysis; modern implementations include the OpenMusic Tonnetz library and the Tonnetz Sequent hardware sequencer. citeturn18search30turn18search6turn18search34 |
| **Geometric chord / voice-leading space** | A chord is a point in a mathematical space; distance corresponds to voice-leading relationships | Search for smooth or structurally interesting transitions geometrically | Dmitri Tymoczko's *A Geometry of Music* builds diagrams translating relationships among chords/scales into spatial geometry. citeturn18search17turn7search7 |
| **Rhythm circle / polygon** | A period is a circle; attacks become points around the circumference | Rotate, distribute and compare rhythms geometrically | Godfried Toussaint's *The Geometry of Musical Rhythm* systematically studies rhythms using computational geometry; related research represents rhythmic onsets as points on a circle. citeturn18search3turn7academia20 |
| **Patch-cord / data-flow graph** | Boxes perform operations; wires carry numbers, MIDI, audio or musical structures | Build the composition process rather than manually enter the finished notes | Max makes object relationships visible through patch cords; Pure Data follows the same visual-programming lineage. citeturn17search0turn17search13 |
| **Computer-assisted composition graph** | Nodes represent transformations, sets, lists, constraints or musical objects rather than audio processors | Build algorithms that output scores/material | IRCAM's **OpenMusic/OM#** is explicitly visual programming for computer-assisted composition, score generation and music/audio-data processing. citeturn23search6turn23search10turn23search14 |
| **Modular signal-flow canvas** | Modules are instruments/processors; cables describe signal and modulation topology | Compose by constructing a dynamic musical machine | VCV Rack recreates modular-patching behavior digitally; its community has thousands of discussion topics devoted to Rack, plugins and patches. citeturn16search2turn16search4 |
| **Draw-the-sound / graphical synthesis** | Drawn position, curves, shapes or pixels map directly into pitch, spectrum, dynamics or time | Literally sketch sound trajectories | Xenakis's **UPIC** made drawing a primary composition input and could control structures from waveforms/envelopes to macro-form. MetaSynth similarly provides a paint-oriented image-synthesis environment. citeturn23search9turn23search17turn17search3 |
| **Graphical / indeterminate score** | Shapes communicate relationships or actions without uniquely specifying conventional notes | Performer interprets graphics according to rules or judgment | Cage's *Notations*, Cardew's *Treatise* and contemporary ScorePlayer practice exemplify this branch. citeturn25search0turn25search38turn24search12 |
| **Animated / dynamic score** | Notation itself changes, scrolls or synchronizes during performance | Compose time-dependent visual instructions impossible on a static page | Decibel ScorePlayer synchronizes predominantly graphic scores across devices for rehearsal/performance. citeturn24search2turn24search20 |
| **Generative spatial playground** | Objects/nodes and their spatial relationships become music generators | Move/connect objects instead of entering notes | NodeBeat explicitly describes itself as a visual music application that can either be composed manually or allowed to generate music. citeturn24search7 |
| **Trajectory / cursor score** | Moving cursors intersect curves or objects and emit events | Draw pathways and let time traverse them | IanniX is an open-source graphical sequencer based on Xenakis's ideas; curves and events can be synchronized to other software over OSC. citeturn17search2turn17search10 |
| **Spectral / image composition** | X is usually time while Y can be frequency/pitch; image intensity/color can control amplitude/timbre | Paint the sonic spectrum | MetaSynth's Image Synth explicitly treats image painting as synthesis and composition. citeturn17search3 |
| **Context/probability-assisted harmonic UI** | Current musical context is shown together with plausible continuations | Software proposes next chords/ideas while user remains in control | Hookpad's Magic Chord analyzes preceding chords and its TheoryTab corpus of 75,000+ analyzed songs to generate contextual suggestions. citeturn5search8turn19view3 |

There is an important distinction across these systems. **Staff notation, piano rolls and trackers primarily represent music that has already been decided.** A chord graph, Tonnetz, mDecks map, Hookpad recommendation system or Nodal graph can instead represent the **space of decisions that remain available**. Max/OpenMusic represent something still more abstract: the **process that will make the decisions**. UPIC/MetaSynth collapse another boundary entirely by allowing the visual mark itself to become sound. This distinction is visible across the respective systems. citeturn19view1turn19view2turn24search26turn23search6turn23search17

## What the popularity data says

The market data suggests three distinct layers.

**Traditional visual composition is enormous.** BandLab reports more than 100 million creators and fans; FL Studio reports millions of creators and has a multi-million-member forum; MuseScore reports millions of users; Flat now advertises more than five million musicians. These interfaces retain relatively familiar metaphors—tracks, piano rolls, grids or notation. citeturn26search1turn16search1turn16search3turn26search0turn26search14

**Theory-aware composition is smaller but already commercially validated.** Hooktheory's 500,000+ users and 1.5 million Hookpad projects are significant because the product deliberately exposes harmonic relationships instead of merely offering a conventional DAW. iReal Pro's 2M+ musicians provide further evidence that large audiences are comfortable working at the level of chord symbols rather than individual notes. citeturn19view3turn13search5

**True spatial/network composition remains fragmented and specialist.** mDecks, Illustrated Harmony, Tonnetz tools, OpenMusic, IanniX, Nodal, UPIC descendants and graphic-score systems all demonstrate powerful ideas, but I found no evidence of a mass consumer product that combines them into a unified graphical songwriting environment. That absence is a research finding, not proof that no obscure product exists. The public systems instead tend to specialize in one representation: functional harmony, chord paths, transformational geometry, algorithmic graphs, generative traversal, drawn synthesis, or indeterminate notation. citeturn19view2turn19view1turn18search6turn23search6turn17search2turn24search26turn23search11

The most promising unexplored design appears to be a **multi-resolution musical graph**. At the highest level, nodes could represent song sections; zooming in could turn them into progression nodes; another level could expose individual voicings or melodic gestures. Edges could be typed rather than generic—**functional resolution, common-tone/voice-leading proximity, modulation, borrowed harmony, repetition, probabilistic recommendation, or user-created relationship**. Playback could traverse one selected path while the surrounding graph continues to show alternatives. This is an inference from the complementary strengths of Illustrated Harmony, mDecks, Tonnetz, Nodal, Tymoczko's chord geometry and Hookpad's data-assisted recommendations. citeturn19view1turn19view2turn18search30turn24search26turn18search17turn5search8

That would solve a problem most current interfaces do not solve simultaneously: a conventional DAW shows **what happened in time**; a score shows **what performers should play**; mDecks shows **where a chord functions tonally**; Tonnetz shows **what is harmonically nearby**; Illustrated Harmony shows **possible routes**; Hookpad shows **contextually plausible continuations**; OpenMusic shows **how musical material is generated**.
