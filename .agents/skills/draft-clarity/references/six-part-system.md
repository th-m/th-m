# Six-Part Draft-Clarity System

This catalog adapts the user-supplied notes from an educational video about
turning exploratory nonfiction into cohesive linear prose. The method applies
to writing that makes a point, answers a question, or solves a problem. It is
not a general standard for fiction, memoir, or poetry.

The governing distinction is between the **briefed writer**, whose background
knowledge silently fills gaps, and the **cold reader**, who has only the words
and sequence on the page.

## 1. Align the Introduction and Conclusion Points

Identify the complete introduction and conclusion even when either spans
several paragraphs. Extract the one sentence, or smallest group of sentences,
that best states the point of each.

Compare them:

- If they make the same claim at different resolutions, preserve the sharper
  language and the introduction's necessary framing.
- If the conclusion advances a meaning the introduction did not prepare, decide
  whether the draft discovered a better thesis or drifted into a different one.
- If either section has no extractable point, mark the alignment Broken rather
  than inventing one on the author's behalf.

Formulate a proposed **working point sentence** from the strongest supported
parts of both. Treat it as a diagnostic instrument until the user authorizes a
revision.

Source-note range: approximately 302.8s–506.9s.

## 2. Choose Point-First or Point-Last

For conventional point-first nonfiction, place or clearly establish the
working point near the end of the introduction. Test whether the introduction
provides every term and distinction required to understand it.

For an intentional point-last design:

- introduce the central concepts or thematic preview early;
- seed the key terms that will carry the final claim;
- preserve enough uncertainty to justify delay without concealing the subject;
- verify that the conclusion creates recognition and closure rather than a new
  argument arriving too late.

Do not misclassify a buried or missing thesis as an advanced point-last design.

Source-note range: approximately 528.5s–643.3s.

## 3. Rebuild Sections Around Reader Questions

Ignore the draft's drafting history. Divide the body into conceptual sections
based on the questions a reader must answer in sequence.

For every section:

1. State the reader question it answers.
2. Extract one to three sentences that state its point.
3. Check whether that point appears in an index position near the beginning.
4. Check whether the preceding material creates a logical transition into it.
5. Check whether its ending prepares the next reader question.

A missing section point usually signals mixed purposes or an undeveloped idea.
Numbered ordering is not evidence of logical sequence. Rhetorical questions can
reset attention when they express the actual next question rather than decorate
the transition.

Source-note range: approximately 729.4s–977.7s.

## 4. Map Point → Reason → Evidence

For each consequential assertion, identify:

- **Point:** the contestable claim the passage asks the reader to accept;
- **Reason:** why the claim should follow, often exposed by adding “because”;
- **Evidence:** the observation, example, quotation, experience, statistic, or
  data that makes the reason credible for the intended audience.

Test both links independently:

```text
evidence supports reason → reason supports point
```

Flag circular reasons, evidence that supports a nearby but different claim,
unstated warrants, and evidence types the target reader is unlikely to accept.
The first broken link is usually more useful than a general request for “more
evidence.”

Source-note range: approximately 1040.2s–1244.7s.

## 5. Trace the Uneven U

The Uneven U tracks a section's movement through levels of specificity:

1. **Point:** begin with a specific, falsifiable assertion.
2. **Explanation:** clarify the assertion and move toward its evidence.
3. **Preparation:** establish the terms, context, or comparison needed to
   understand the evidence.
4. **Evidence:** present the concrete case, quotation, statistic, or observation.
5. **Interpretation:** explain what the evidence shows and reconnect it to the
   point.
6. **Resolution:** end at a broader level than the opening by connecting the
   sub-point to the thesis or next question.

The shape is diagnostic, not a paragraph template. Some moves can be compressed
or repeated. Flag vacuous opening claims, surprising evidence without setup,
hit-and-run evidence without interpretation, and endings that merely restate
the opening instead of widening its significance.

Source-note range: approximately 1312.1s–1597.4s.

## 6. Calibrate Certainty

Match claim strength to evidence strength and subject scope.

**Stridency indicators** include universal or self-validating language such as
“every,” “only,” “obviously,” and “of course” when the draft has not earned that
scope. Diagnose the unsupported leap, not the word in isolation.

**Hedging indicators** include stacks of modal and softening phrases that make
the claim impossible to test. Determine whether uncertainty is epistemically
necessary or merely protective.

Prefer two repairs:

- choose a precise main verb that expresses the actual degree of certainty;
- narrow the subject noun until the claim's scope matches its support.

Preserve uncertainty required by the evidence. Confidence comes from a bounded,
well-supported claim rather than stronger tone.

Source-note range: approximately 1818.9s–2069.8s.
