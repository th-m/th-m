# Nine-Technique Clarity Catalog

This catalog adapts the user-supplied notes from “9 Techniques for Clearer
Writing (I Wish I Knew Sooner).” It groups the techniques at sentence,
paragraph, and idea levels. Treat the patterns as diagnostic options rather
than mandatory templates.

## Sentence Level

### 1. Put Main Characters in Subject Position

Identify who or what the sentence is chiefly about. Check whether that character
occupies the grammatical subject position, where readers naturally look for the
center of attention.

Flag sentences whose main character is buried in a possessive phrase,
prepositional phrase, abstraction, or passive construction when that placement
obscures responsibility. Preserve other arrangements when the affected entity
or continuity of topic deserves the subject position.

Source-note range: approximately 161.0s–176.6s.

### 2. Express Main Actions in Verbs

Identify the action that carries the sentence's meaning. Prefer a specific verb
when that action is hidden in a nominalization and supported only by a weak verb
such as “is,” “has,” “makes,” or “performs.”

Test the repair as a character-action pair. A stronger verb should reveal who
does what, not merely shorten the sentence or intensify its tone.

Source-note range: approximately 197.8s–305.7s.

### 3. Use Nominalizations Strategically

Nominalizations can improve clarity when they:

- compress a previous clause into familiar old information;
- replace an awkward noun clause such as “the fact that…”;
- turn an established action or idea into a concrete object receiving an action;
- name a concept already familiar enough to the audience to function as a
  character, such as a recognized domain term.

Scrutinize nominalizations in subject position when they hide the actor or force
the reader to unpack an unfamiliar clause-sized idea before reaching the verb.
Judge the reader effect, not the suffix.

Source-note range: approximately 331.2s–585.4s.

## Paragraph Level

### 4. Create Cohesive Sentence Handoffs

Trace the important information near the end of one sentence into the beginning
of the next. A useful handoff can repeat a term, use a pronoun, or compress the
prior clause into a familiar noun phrase.

Passive voice can help when it places the prior sentence's object into the next
sentence's subject position. Flag a handoff only when the reader must make an
unprepared jump.

Source-note range: approximately 705.6s–897.2s.

### 5. Create Coherence with Consistent Subjects

Underline the grammatical subject or roughly the first seven to eight words of
each sentence. Read them as a sequence. They should name a small, related set of
important characters that represents the paragraph's topic.

Distinguish deliberate topic progression from random subject switching. A
coherent paragraph can change subjects when the relationship among them remains
visible.

Source-note range: approximately 933.2s–1263.8s.

### 6. Move from Simple to Complex and Old to New

Give the reader an early foothold: a short introductory phrase when needed, a
short familiar subject, and a specific action. Place long phrases, subordinate
clauses, unfamiliar terminology, and the sentence's main new information toward
the end when meaning permits.

Do not move complexity mechanically. Preserve the order required by chronology,
contrast, causality, or emphasis.

Source-note range: approximately 1313.4s–1491.5s.

## Idea Level

### 7. Establish a Problem Readers Care About

For problem-driven nonfiction, test the introduction for this progression:

1. **Status quo:** the existing view, condition, or practice.
2. **Concession:** what is reasonable or valuable about it.
3. **Destabilizing condition:** what the status quo cannot explain or accomplish.
4. **Cost or benefit:** why the unresolved problem matters to this reader.
5. **Idea:** the thesis presented as a response or solution.

This is a diagnostic sequence, not mandatory phrasing. Do not invent a false
opponent, exaggerated crisis, or unsupported cost to force the pattern.

Source-note range: approximately 1605.7s–1869.8s.

### 8. Signal the Big Picture

Check four coherence signals:

- headings or visible boundaries identify conceptual sections;
- each major unit opens with a short introductory segment;
- the unit's point appears by the end of that segment;
- important terms from the point recur as the unit develops them.

Repetition should preserve conceptual identity. Avoid unnecessary synonyms that
make the reader wonder whether a new concept has appeared.

Source-note range: approximately 1962.9s–2200.7s.

### 9. Use Index and Discussion at Every Useful Scale

An **index** is a short, graspable opening that states the unit's point and names
its key concepts. A **discussion** develops those concepts with the necessary
complexity.

Inspect the pattern at the scales present in the text:

- sentence: short subject or main clause, then fuller predicate or support;
- paragraph: orienting point sentence, then development;
- section: short opening segment, then detailed discussion;
- document: concise introduction, then body.

The pattern is fractal but not compulsory at every boundary. Use it where the
reader needs orientation before complexity, and preserve deliberate variation.

Source-note range: approximately 2281.6s–2603.2s.
